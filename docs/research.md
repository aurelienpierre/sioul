# What the research says, and the rule each finding gives

The detailed notes behind these rules (what each study found, how strong its evidence is, where Sioul applies it, and what was built, left for later or refused, with why) are in [research/README.md](research/README.md).

The evidence is not all of one strength: randomised trials and meta-analyses, observational studies, qualitative studies with the people concerned, guidelines, and, where no study tested the point, Sioul's own inference, marked as such in the notes. What none of it shows yet is that Sioul, as built, lightens admin for the people who use it. The research supports the design, not yet its outcome: whether letters are avoided less, starting comes easier, mail intrudes less, fewer things are forgotten and evenings recover better needs testing with the people who use it.

## Who the findings are about
Sioul is designed for the people for whom admin costs most. Each group below has the findings that concern it most; most findings serve several groups, and none needs a diagnosis to apply.
- **Autistic people**: uncertainty (5), autistic inertia (6), cognitive accessibility (10), visual supports (13), autistic burnout (19), the phone and executive function in admin (29), overwhelm, meltdown and shutdown (32), unexpected calls (34).
- **People with ADHD**: support at the point of performance (6), criticism (15), timers (18), "brain weather" (19), timing (24), admin and executive function (29), the two routes of ADHD and PTSD (30), working for oneself (31).
- **People living with PTSD or another trauma**: trauma-informed design (9), an office as a reminder of harm (30), what helps and harms in overwhelm (32).
- **Anxiety**: avoidance as mood repair (4), uncertainty (5), admin anxiety (30), unexpected calls (34).
- **Depression**: shame and guilt (23), timing (24), valued activities as a dose (27), daylight and time outdoors (33).
- **Burnout and exhaustion**: autistic burnout (19), detachment from work (21), unfinished tasks (22), recovery (26).
- **ME/CFS, long COVID and other illnesses that limit energy**: pacing within the energy envelope (25), the crash after exertion (33), the sides of what an activity costs (36).
- **Eating disorders and irregular eating**: meals by the clock, set by the person (28).
- **Everyone who knows admin anxiety**: administrative burden (3), avoidance (4), admin anxiety and its routes (30), worry as a cost of its own (36), the portal model (37), accounts looked away from (39), reminders that name the bill (40), red and accounting words (43).
- **People who work for themselves**: the admin it adds (31), taxes set aside and weeks of cover (44).
- **People with irregular or tight income** (paid by the week, by benefits or by clients): the pay cycle (38), forecasts that forget expenses (42), how long the money lasts (44).
- **Parents who share a child's care, in one home or two**: several agendas (46), one person in two places (47), the noticing and reminding (48), a holder from end to end (49), parents in parallel (50), records that invite score-keeping (51), availability kept private (52), one event in two agendas (54).
- **People who share their time with a partner, or with several**: several agendas (46), availability kept private (52), a bare "busy" (53).

## The findings

1. **Fewer, predictable looks at mail.**
   - Checking mail three times a day for a week, against freely, lowered daily stress in a randomised trial of 124 adults; lower stress predicted better well-being (Kushlev & Dunn 2015).
   - Batching notifications three times a day improved attention, mood, productivity and stress in 237 participants, while those given no notifications at all felt *more* anxious than controls (Fitz et al. 2019).
   - Without email for five days, 13 office workers switched windows half as often, and their heart-rate variability showed less stress (Mark, Voida & Cardello 2012).
   - **Rule**: admin windows you choose, and the app always says when the next one opens. Predictability, not silence.
2. **Interruptions cost even when ignored.** Interrupted people work faster, with more stress, frustration and effort (Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008). A notification left unanswered still disrupts attention (Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert 2015). **Rule**: no pop-ups, sounds or badges; the layout never moves under your eyes.
3. **Administrative burden** is made of learning, compliance and psychological costs (Moynihan, Herd & Harvey 2015). It looms larger for people with fewer cognitive resources, executive function, scarcity and health problems among them, as Christensen et al. (2020) argue, naming ADHD; a test followed: in two surveys, people reporting ADHD, anxiety or chronic pain found tax or student-aid rules more burdensome, and those reporting ADHD or pain lost the aid more often for missed requirements (Bell et al. 2023; observational, small effects). **Rule**: Sioul carries the three costs. Learning: what a letter is and wants. Compliance: drafts, attachments and addresses gathered. Psychological: tone, pacing, no shame.
4. **Avoidance is mood repair, not laziness.** Procrastination gives priority to short-term mood (Sirois & Pychyl 2013), and people avoid information they expect to hurt (Sweeny et al. 2010; Golman, Hagmann & Loewenstein 2017). **Rule**: make looking cheaper. The card comes first, saying who, what it asks, by when, and what happens if nothing is done.
5. **Uncertainty fuels anxiety** (Carleton 2012), in autism too (Boulter et al. 2014). **Rule**: every item states what happens next and who waits on whom; no ambiguous states.
6. **Starting is the hard part.**
   - Autistic inertia is a difficulty acting on intentions, eased by scaffolding: "no way out except from external intervention" (Buckle et al. 2021).
   - For ADHD, the support belongs at the point of performance: time, memory and motivation externalised (Barkley 2012). Tools still leave prioritisation, time estimation and task switching to ADHD knowledge workers (Campbell, Çerçi & Cecchinato 2024).
   - "When X, I do Y" plans raise follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006); near subgoals build competence (Bandura & Schunk 1981).
   - **Rule**: one next step, small, with a when, pre-filled where possible; priorities computed by Sioul.
7. **Company helps.** Body doubling is a practice neurodivergent people found by themselves (Eagle & Ringland 2024). **Rule**: an admin session can have a companion, an AI or a person.
8. **Progress, never streaks.** Small wins carry motivation (Amabile & Kramer 2011). Broken streaks lower later engagement, more so when people blame themselves (Silverman & Barasch 2023). **Rule**: show what got done; no streaks, no pile of "overdue".
9. **Trauma-informed design: expect that trauma may be present, and do not repeat it.**
   - SAMHSA's guidance for services asks an organisation to realise how widespread trauma is, recognise its signs, respond by building that knowledge into its procedures, and resist re-traumatisation, through six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; cultural, historical and gender issues. It takes "a universal precautions approach in which one expects the presence of trauma in lives of individuals being served, ensuring not to replicate it", and asks services to keep people informed of procedures and schedules, "mindful that people who are frightened or overwhelmed may have a difficulty processing information" (SAMHSA 2014, pp. 9–11 and 15).
   - Chen et al. (2022) adapt the principles to computing (safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, enablement, intersectionality): design as if trauma may be at play for anyone, so that nobody has to disclose it; warn before visible changes; explain why something is shown; rethink harsh warnings that force attention; let no assistant judge users or raise their anxiety.
   - Frameworks and design cases: strong as a shared vocabulary, untested as to effect. SAMHSA writes for services, and says nothing of software or paperwork: applying it to admin software is Chen et al.'s step, and Sioul's.
   - **Rule**, principle by principle:
     - *safety*: no surprises: no badge, no red, new mail without a sound, nothing moving under your eyes; forged mail marked by a shape and a word, not an alarm ([design.md](design.md));
     - *trust and transparency*: the reason shown (why this step now, why this mail was set aside, what a setting does), anything moved, deleted or sent said in one sentence, with ten seconds to undo;
     - *collaboration*: Sioul proposes and you decide: nothing sent, deleted or paid without you, not even by an AI agent;
     - *enablement, voice and choice*: your rules and your hours; your own word for your state (a day clear, hazy or foggy), never inferred; each field shown when it is needed;
     - *cultural, historical and gender issues*: Sioul asks nothing about who you are, needs no diagnosis and infers nothing, so nothing has to be disclosed;
     - *peer support* is not software's to give; making room for the people who help, a project handed to a helper with what it needs, is not built yet ([research/life-admin.md](research/life-admin.md), §3); each project is opened to AI agents on its own, closed until then ([ai.md](ai.md)).
10. **Cognitive accessibility** (W3C, 2021): help users focus; make processes not rely on memory; use clear language; help users avoid mistakes; support personalisation. For autistic users (UK Home Office, 2016): simple colours, plain English, short sentences and bullets, descriptive buttons, simple and consistent layouts. **Rule**: key hints on screen, literal labels, confirmations before anything irreversible, everything adjustable.
11. **Tracking is work too.** Self-tracking imposes "substantial interpretive and emotional demands" on neurodivergent people (Rudberg Selin, Unéus & Knudsen 2026). **Rule**: nothing to log by obligation.
12. **AI as a communication aid, with care.** Autistic workers preferred an LLM to a human for workplace communication advice, while a job coach found some of that advice questionable (Jang et al. 2024). **Rule**: the AI drafts and explains, you review, and it never sends.
13. **Visual supports** are an evidence-based practice in autism, mostly studied in children and young people (Steinbrenner et al. 2020). **Rule**: timelines and the Gantt chart on demand, never compulsory.
14. **Too many options weigh when choosing is hard.** Across 50 experiments the mean effect of more options was nil (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd 2010); overload appears when options are hard to compare, preferences unclear and effort to be saved (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman 2015): a to-do list on a tired day. **Rule**: one next step, picked, with its reason; equal steps are picked, not shown as a choice ([tasks.md](tasks.md)).
15. **A plan date is not a deadline, and lateness is not a debt.** Forgiving oneself for procrastinating lowered later procrastination (Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett 2010); adults with ADHD meet more criticism, and lower self-compassion goes with poorer mental health (Beaton, Sirois & Milne 2022). **Rule**: nothing overdue; a day to start that passed says nothing; the date asked is time left; no counts of what was put off.
16. **Once started, a task is rarely as bad as feared**, and an interrupted task pulls to be resumed (Pychyl's experience sampling; Ghibellini & Meier 2025). **Rule**: a two-minute start where stopping counts.
17. **A plan for where you stopped frees the mind for the next thing.** Writing a one-minute ready-to-resume plan reduced attention residue (Leroy & Glomb 2018); a warning before an interruption shortens getting back in (Trafton et al. 2003). **Rule**: stopping offers one line, shown when the task comes back; a heads-up two minutes before the end.
18. **Time made visible**: visual timers and day schedules improved time management in children with ADHD in a randomised trial (Wennberg et al. 2018); a ticking countdown can raise anxiety instead. **Rule**: a draining disc in a neutral colour, without ticking or sound, open-ended when you prefer.
19. **Capacity is lower on some days, and only you know which.** Autistic burnout brings chronic exhaustion and loss of executive skills, eased by reduced expectations (Raymaker et al. 2020); adults with ADHD liked a "brain weather" view and disliked tools that watch them (Chen, Meng & Nie 2026). **Rule**: the day's weather is chosen, never inferred; fog shows small steps, and one is a full day.
20. **Splitting a task makes it startable and its estimate truer** (Kruger & Evans 2004). **Rule**: steps in one line each, their minutes added up.
21. **Work cues in the evening undo recovery.** Detachment from work goes with less exhaustion (r = −.38 across 91 samples: Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017), and the most exhausted detach least (Sonnentag et al. 2014). Work messages in the evening cost the evening through their tone (Butts, Becker & Boswell 2015), and the mere expectation of checking harms, read or not (Becker et al. 2021). **Rule**: quiet time outside working hours, during time off and once the day is closed: only the senders you let through then, codes and what you enjoy, with one sentence saying until when; during sleep, no notification but doses ([porch.md](porch.md), [areas.md](areas.md)).
22. **An unfinished task stops pulling once it has a place.** Unfinished tasks feed affective rumination and poor sleep (Syrek et al. 2017; Wendsche, Weigelt & Syrek 2026); writing where, when and how they will be done quieted intrusions in the lab (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011, not replicated) and raised evening detachment in the field, mostly for people low in self-control (Smit 2016). **Rule**: closing the day gives everything a place and names the first step, concretely.
23. **Shame, not guilt, feeds avoidance, and repair makes it constructive.** Shame tracks depression more than guilt does, as does guilt over what one could not control (Kim, Thibodeau & Jorgensen 2011); shame turns to repair when repair looks possible (Leach & Cidam 2015); feedback about the self rather than the task lowers performance (Kluger & DeNisi 1996). Warm affirmations can threaten the most self-critical (Kirby, Day & Sagar 2019). **Rule**: no count of what was not done, no history of early stops, no question; kindness in what the software does, in plain words about tasks.
24. **Your guess of how long a task takes carries information, with a bias.** The typical error is 20–30 %: short tasks are overestimated, long ones underestimated, and a day's total overruns because overruns are bigger than underruns (Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen 2012). Guessed and real times correlated at r = .77 despite a 1.6× bias (Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994). People misremember how long past tasks took, and seeing the measured times of similar ones helps (Roy, Christenfeld & McKenzie 2005; Roy, Mitten & Christenfeld 2008). ADHD and depression change timing over seconds, not the length predicted for a task (Marx et al. 2022; Thönes & Oberfeld 2015). **Rule**: your guess is kept and corrected, never replaced: time spent against time guessed, as a ratio on a log scale, over about two weeks, per kind of task pulled toward your overall ratio; the safety margin held for the whole day, not per task; never a score, never "late", never your estimate pre-filled, never a diagnosis. Detail: [time-estimation.md](research/time-estimation.md). Built: [capacity.md](capacity.md), the kind being the task's area.
25. **An average of past load measures habit, not capacity.** In sports, the long-term load average predicted injury no better than random numbers (Impellizzeri et al. 2021); one session more than 10 % above the longest of the past 30 days raised injury risk (Frandsen et al. 2025). In ME/CFS, activity is never raised automatically, only after a stable period, and a crash can come days later (NICE NG206, 2021); in long COVID, a structured pacing protocol was followed by fewer crashes in an uncontrolled cohort (Parker et al. 2023). **Rule**: a daily budget for each cost (thinking, feelings, anxiety, body and senses) and in total, learned from days that went well, with the outcome you give at the end of the day ([reviews.md](reviews.md)); raised slowly after stable days, lowered at once after a crash; no day planned above the heaviest well-tolerated one plus 10 %; no gauge. Detail: [capacity-budget.md](research/capacity-budget.md). Built: [capacity.md](capacity.md).
26. **Willpower is not a tank; recovery is.** Large replications found ego depletion near zero (Hagger et al. 2016; Vohs et al. 2021); breaks of more than ten minutes after demanding tasks help (Albulescu et al. 2022). **Rule**: the plan counts the day's load and its recovery, not minutes of willpower; after a heavy block, a light one or a real break, never two heavy blocks in a row. Built: [capacity.md](capacity.md).
27. **What gives back is a floor to reach, not a currency.** Scheduling valued activities works as a dose (Jacobson et al. 1996; a self-guided app in a 649-person trial, Dahne et al. 2025); good events raise good mood without lowering bad mood (David et al. 1997), so a gain does not buy room for more cost. Free time helps up to about two hours a day and less past five (Sharif, Mogilner & Hershfield 2021); measuring enjoyment lowers it (Etkin 2016). What gives back: detachment, relaxation, autonomy, mastery, meaning, company (DRAMMA, Kujanpää et al. 2021). **Rule**: one rating of what it gives back, 0 to 10, given afterwards and rarely; a daily floor kept apart from the costs; something short that gives back right after a heavy block, the evening kept low-key; Sioul keeps the slot free, you choose what goes in it; never a quota. Detail: [wellbeing-gain.md](research/wellbeing-gain.md). Built: [capacity.md](capacity.md).
28. **Meals by the clock, set by you, said plainly**, for people with an eating disorder and for people who eat irregularly (burnout, depression, ADHD). Regular eating by the clock, not by hunger (CBT-E: Murphy et al. 2010); every part set by the person (Lindgreen, Lomborg & Clausen 2018); neutral words, no numbers, no "missed" (Eikey 2021; Levinson et al. 2017). **Rule**: meals, naps and the night are set first and kept free, the work goes in what is left; two notices at most; moved or skipped for today without a word asked; nothing about food, portions or numbers ever recorded or shown. Detail: [meal-prompts.md](research/meal-prompts.md). Built: [health.md](health.md), "Meals, rest and sleep".
29. **Admin weighs more on neurodivergent people, through executive function and the phone.**
   - ADHD's own diagnostic criteria name admin: forms and long papers avoided; calls, bills and appointments forgotten; paperwork lost (American Psychiatric Association 2013). Its executive weaknesses (inhibition, working memory, planning) are of medium size (Willcutt et al. 2005), and procrastination rises with inattention across 11 studies (Suriano 2026). Adults with ADHD notice incoming bills less (Koerts et al. 2023), miss more GP appointments (odds ratio 1.9 in Scottish records: McQueenie et al. 2024), and default on credit more and more from middle age (Swedish registers: Beauchaine, Ben-David & Bos 2020).
   - Most autistic adults avoid the phone (78 % against 38 %), and 62 % find booking a GP appointment by phone hard, against 16 % (Doherty et al. 2022); for services they rank e-mail first and the phone last (Howard & Sedgewick 2021); a third cannot process speech fast enough for real-time talk (Raymaker et al. 2017).
   - Support workers in Sweden say that young adults with autism or ADHD often need help with authorities, calls and documents, and that sorting out their contacts with authorities often comes first (Löthberg et al. 2024).
   - **Rule**: the software carries the dates, notices the bills and the debits, and turns each letter into one small step; writing before calls ([research/life-admin.md](research/life-admin.md), §1.3–1.4; [research/call-screening.md](research/call-screening.md)).
30. **Admin anxiety is common, and ADHD and PTSD make admin heavier by different routes; no study shows a dread of offices that the two share.**
   - "Admin anxiety", a term from a UK policy institute's surveys (grey literature), was found in 23 % of UK adults and 37 % of people with mental health problems, with banks and utilities; the telephone was the hardest channel, text and the web the easiest, and some never open the post they dread (Holkar, Evans & Langston 2018). With benefits, avoiding contact with the agencies was common (Bond, Braverman & Evans 2019). Neither breaks its results down by diagnosis.
   - With ADHD, the route runs through forgetting, avoided effort and procrastination (finding 29).
   - With PTSD, avoidance is of reminders of the trauma (World Health Organization 2024, ICD-11), and an office becomes one when it makes people tell or prove the trauma again: veterans and people in therapy for trauma described benefit assessments and disability claims as feared and re-traumatising (Scullion & Curchin 2022; Roberts et al. 2024; Webermann et al. 2026). Harm done by an institution goes with distrust of it (Smith & Freyd 2014). PTSD also comes with weaker attention, memory and executive function (Scott et al. 2015).
   - The two often come together: PTSD is about three times as frequent with ADHD, and ADHD 1.7 times as frequent with PTSD (Spencer et al. 2016, meta-analysis), though not significantly more than in people with other psychiatric conditions.
   - No study measures a dread of letters, forms or calls from offices in people with ADHD or with PTSD, nor compares the two on admin.
   - **Rule**: serve both routes. Against forgetting and avoided effort: the card first, saying who, what it asks, by when and what happens if nothing is done; one small step; every date carried by Sioul. Against threat: no surprises, writing before calls, and papers kept once, to be given again without retelling ([papers.md](papers.md)). For both: nothing that shames, nothing overdue.
31. **Working for oneself adds admin, and people with a disability or a long-term condition work for themselves more often.**
   - In the US in 2025, 9.1 % of employed people with a disability were self-employed, against 5.9 % of those without (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2026); European data point the same way (Pagán 2009; Kitching 2014). ADHD symptoms went with self-employment in a Swedish population cohort, modestly and through hyperactivity (odds ratio 1.13: Verheul et al. 2016), and a reported diagnosis with plans to start a business, and with starting one, among Dutch students (Lerner, Verheul & Thurik 2019). No study gives a rate for autistic adults. In interviews, some disabled and autistic people left jobs that would not adapt to them (Adams et al. 2019; Davies et al. 2025).
   - The self-employed declare and pay their own taxes, where an employer does it for employees (Milanez & Bratta 2019); compliance costs weigh most on the smallest businesses (Evans 2003); none of 14 founders with ADHD was at ease with bookkeeping (Wiklund, Patzelt & Dimov 2016), and disabled self-employed people who find planning hard called tax planning "especially difficult" (Adams et al. 2019).
   - **Rule**: carry the small business's admin too, from the client's first mail to the paid invoice and the figures for the accountant, in the same calm window ([projects.md](projects.md), [accounting.md](accounting.md)).
32. **In overwhelm, less: nothing to answer, nothing new, words that are literal.** Acute stress narrows working memory and flexibility (meta-analysis: Shields, Sazma & Yonelinas 2016), and much of what is said is forgotten at once (Kessels 2003); in an autistic shutdown, speech and movement can stop (Paris et al. 2025). Psychological first aid, the consensus for the first minutes, brings immediate relief and does not prevent PTSD (World Health Organization et al. 2011; one trial); single-session debriefing helped nobody, and made PTSD more likely at one year in one trial (Rose et al. 2002, Cochrane); trying to relax under load raises arousal, so "calm down" backfires (Wegner, Broome & Blumberg 1997). No study has tested a pause button. **Rule**: the Pause asks nothing and shows nothing new: set up beforehand, one press, a few literal sentences about Sioul and never about the person, no question, a breathing guide only if chosen, nothing sent to anyone, the emergency number and a crisis line one tap away; coming back, the rest of the day is lighter ([pauses.md](pauses.md); detail: [emergency-pause.md](research/emergency-pause.md), criteria P1–P31).
33. **Taking the good moment: going out into it, briefly, with a say over one's own working time, within the energy envelope.** Pleasant weather lifted mood and memory only in people who spent more than half an hour outside (Keller et al. 2005); green exercise gave most of its effect early, the largest gain in self-esteem at five minutes (Barton & Pretty 2010); each extra hour a day outdoors in daylight went with less low mood, easier waking and fewer insomnia symptoms in about 400,000 adults (Burns et al. 2021). Control over one's own working time improves the balance between work and the rest of life (Nijp et al. 2012, 53 studies), while work in the evening costs detachment and sleep (finding 21). In ME/CFS and long COVID, exertion can bring a crash that starts within a day and peaks within three (Stussman et al. 2020; Appelman et al. 2024). **Rule**: Free time is one press: work waits, only the people you chose reach you, and the work day may end later by as much, never into the wind-down nor past a latest end; movement is offered as a choice, never with "Days kept even", the setting for energy-limiting illness ([pauses.md](pauses.md); detail: [global-pause.md](research/global-pause.md), criteria GP1–GP21).
34. **The phone is the hardest channel, and an expected call is easier than a surprise.** 78 % of autistic adults avoid the phone where they can, and 61 % avoid voicemail too (Doherty et al. 2022); people with admin anxiety find the telephone the hardest channel (Holkar, Evans & Langston 2018); intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety go together in autism (r = 0.62: Jenkinson, Milne & Thompson 2020); text reminders keep appointments as well as calls do (Gurol-Urganci et al. 2013). No study tests call screening itself: the case rests on stated preferences and a plausible mechanism. **Rule**: on a phone, calls follow the same rules as mail: a call that may not reach you now is declined plainly and goes to voicemail, and the Porch says afterwards who called; Sioul never answers and never listens; the emergency services calling back, and a second call within fifteen minutes, always ring ([attention.md](attention.md); detail: [call-screening.md](research/call-screening.md), criteria CS1–CS21).
35. **A spam filter learns best from one's own mail, and the labels one gives matter more than the model.** Filters trained on other people's mail did about a hundred times worse than filters learning inside one mailbox, in the blind tests of the TREC spam tracks (Cormack 2008; Sculley & Wachman 2007); labelling about 1 % of one's mail near the threshold gave 20 to 150 times less missed spam than the provider's own filter (Cormack & Mojdeh 2009); and a pass of SPF, DKIM or DMARC is almost no evidence, since 89 % of unwanted mail passes (Cloudflare 2023). **Rule**: Sioul's filter learns on your computer, from your own mail and your own word; it judges strangers' mail only, never people you know, codes or projects; until you choose, it moves nothing ([spam-filter.md](spam-filter.md); detail: [research/spam-filter.md](research/spam-filter.md)).

36. **What an activity costs has several sides, and a rating needs words.**
   - NICE's guideline for ME/CFS says that energy management "includes all types of activity (cognitive, physical, emotional and social)", and asks that the plan weigh the "effect of environmental factors, including sensory stimulation" (NICE NG206, 2021, recommendations 1.11.2 and 1.11.3; the ME Association reproduces the section in full). Thinking, feelings, and the body and the senses are three of those sides.
   - Admin adds worry: its burden weighs more on people with ADHD, anxiety or chronic pain (finding 3: Christensen et al. 2020; Bell et al. 2023), and dread runs before and after the task itself ([capacity-budget.md](research/capacity-budget.md), criterion 2).
   - A single rating from 0 to 10, with words at fixed numbers, is Borg's CR10 scale (Borg 1982). Session-RPE rates a whole training session on it, and the rating times the minutes tracked a heart-rate load closely (Foster et al. 2001).
   - **Rule**: four costs, thinking, feelings, worry, and body and senses, and a gain, each from 0 to 10 and unsaid until said; CR10's words beside the number; in each, a concrete question, feelings asked about through what stirs them. Built: [capacity.md](capacity.md#the-form).

37. **Portals move the work onto the person and gather everyone's data in one store.**
   - Mail is encrypted between servers only when both agree, and can be downgraded to clear text (RFC 3207; RFC 8461; Durumeric et al. 2015, in 2015 data); the GDPR asks for security appropriate to the risk, encryption among its first measures (art. 32), and the CNIL calls everyday channels such as mail "rarely" safe for personal data without more (2024). Organisations answered with portals.
   - The Défenseur des droits found that dematerialisation moved work and responsibility onto people, following mail "sur une ou plusieurs messageries" included, and that a personal space not consulted in time risks a "rupture des droits" (2022, pp. 23, 58–59); in its 2025 report (April 2026), 61 % of people reported difficulties with their administrative procedures in 2024 (39 % in 2016), authentication systems "changent sans cesse", and without help from family or friends nearly one in four gives up rights from lost time, discouragement or anxiety. Digital self-service makes citizens do caseworkers' tasks, and can also lighten their burden (Madsen, Lindgren & Melin 2022; qualitative).
   - Remembering passwords and transcribing codes are "a very high or impossible burden" for some people with cognitive disabilities; password managers are a named remedy (W3C, WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8; COGA 2021). ADHD, depression, PTSD and burnout come, on average, with weaker working memory, planning or attention (finding 29; Willcutt et al. 2005; Snyder 2013; Scott et al. 2015; Deligkaris et al. 2014).
   - The stores behind portals were breached from 2021 to 2026, often through compromised or usurped logins, or through providers (AP-HP's file-sharing service, the Assurance Maladie's professional portal, a provider of Pôle emploi, Viamedis and Almerys, France Travail and Cap emploi, FICOBA, France Titres, Vacaf); the CNIL counted twice as many breaches affecting over a million people in 2024 as the year before (CNIL 2025).
   - No study measures portal use, or missed portal messages, in people with these conditions: the link is inferred from the burden and executive-function findings, and stated as such.
   - **Rule**: carry the portals in one place meanwhile (Sites: each logged in once, its news held for the person's hours and said in one line on the Porch), and support what lets letters come back to mail end-to-end encrypted: OpenPGP with Autocrypt and the Web Key Directory, keys on an OpenPGP card (on computers; not yet on a phone), a password manager, FIDO2 keys. The person has one window; no organisation needs one store. Detail: [research/secure-mailboxes.md](research/secure-mailboxes.md).

38. **Money runs by the pay cycle, not by the calendar month.**
   - Spending rises when income arrives, even when it was expected, and bills are paid then: after the Social Security check (Stephens 2003; expenditure diaries); over the food-stamp month, as calories fell by 10–15 % (Shapiro 2005); for at least four days after payday, in the app data of about 75,000 people (Gelman et al. 2014). When pay came late, people delayed their mortgage and card payments (Gelman et al. 2020; a natural experiment).
   - People who budget set its period by their pay: 52.7 % of those paid weekly budget by the week, against 24.6 % of all (Zhang et al. 2022; a representative survey of 3,826 adults).
   - **Rule**: the budgets page counts from one income to the next: whether the account holds every payment until then, what is left for everyday spending, and the lowest point; a budget's period may start on its income's day. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.1 and §2.3.

39. **People look away from their accounts when it goes badly, and facts that come unasked help.**
   - Investors looked at their accounts less after markets fell (Karlsson, Loewenstein & Seppi 2009; Sicherman et al. 2016, 1.1 million savers), and people logged in less as their overdrafts grew (Olafsson & Pagel 2017; account data, a working paper); some look more instead (Gherzi et al. 2014).
   - Enrolling customers in overdraft alerts automatically cut overdraft charges by 25 % across 1.5 million customers of six UK banks (Caflisch et al. 2018; bank data); inviting them to sign up reached about one in ten and lowered nothing (FCA 2018; a field trial).
   - **Rule**: a shortfall on an account reaches the person unasked, once, in words, at the next admin window, whatever budgets the hour shows. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.2 and §2.4.

40. **A reminder works when it names the bill and knows the balance; repetition harms.**
   - Reminders that named what the money was for raised savings by about 16 %, and others had no effect (Karlan et al. 2016; field trials); a text three days before an instalment did as much as a cut in interest (Cadena & Schoar 2011; a field trial, a working paper); reminders that ignored the balance cut late fees by 14 % and raised overdraft fees by 9 % (Medina 2021; a field trial).
   - When unwell, 74 % of people with mental health problems put off paying bills (Evans & Acton 2017, from a survey of about 5,500 people; grey literature); among people behind on payments, a quarter were contacted every day or two, and 49 % felt harassed (Murray & Bond 2023; a survey, grey literature).
   - **Rule**: a payment the person makes by hand, or an invoice to send, has one reminder a few days ahead that names it and says whether the account will hold it; an automatic debit has none, unless the account will not hold it. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.4.

41. **Budgets are the person's own labelled accounts, and what is atypical escapes them.**
   - People keep money in mental accounts labelled by source and use (Thaler 1985, theory; Thaler 1999, review); over 90 % of those who budget use categories, from two to one per utility (Zhang et al. 2022); purchases atypical of a category are less often counted against it (Heath & Soll 1996; three studies), and ambiguous ones go to the account with room (Cheema & Soman 2006; experiments).
   - Money earmarked and set apart is kept: savings sealed in two envelopes held better than in one over 14 weeks (Soman & Cheema 2011; a field trial with 146 households); a box earmarked for emergencies helped, one for a narrower use cost liquidity and lowered savings (Dupas & Robinson 2013; a field trial).
   - **Rule**: budgets are the kinds the person makes; Sioul files every movement, the atypical ones too; moving one is the person's choice, its effect said and the payee's next movements asked about once; money set aside stays reachable. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.1.

42. **People forecast their income and forget their expenses, the unusual ones most.**
   - Forecasts of one's own spare money weighed changes in income 2.9 times more than changes in expenses (Berman et al. 2016; 27 studies); a savings goal lowered the forecast, not the spending (Peetz & Buehler 2009); people spent nearly twice what they expected on exceptional expenses (Sussman & Alter 2012), and predictions anchored on a typical week, about $100 short, until people were asked why this week might differ (Howard et al. 2022; ten experiments, 6,044 people).
   - A forecast that shows its uncertainty improved lay people's decisions and kept their trust after it erred (Joslyn & LeClerc 2012; a lab trial; on displays, Kay et al. 2016 and Fernandes et al. 2018).
   - **Rule**: Sioul makes the forecast from the data, never from the person's guess: "What is coming" lists every movement expected, income as plainly as bills; the past twelve months' unusual spending is a line of its own; an uncertain income is said with its condition; at most one chart, which the sentences never need. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.6.

43. **Red darkens expectations, and accounting words are not learned.**
   - Shown in red rather than black, the same financial data lowered risk-taking by 15–30 % and made losses seem lasting (Bazley, Cronqvist & Mormann 2021; eight experiments).
   - Financial education explained about 0.1 % of financial behaviour and faded within 20 months (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014; a meta-analysis of 201 studies); in 45 budgeting apps, the words for accounts and transactions overlapped and confused (Alenazi & Sas 2023; a review of apps).
   - **Rule**: each budget is said in plain words, what is paid, what is still to come, what is left, with no percentage, colour or bar; never debit, credit, cleared or pending. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.5 and §4.

44. **With irregular income, what matters is how long the money lasts.**
   - Monthly income varied by 39 % over a year of diaries kept by 235 US households, with two or three months a year more than 25 % below the household's average (Hannagan & Morduch 2015; a diary study); in bank data on about six million families, getting through an income dip and an expense spike together took about six weeks of take-home pay, which 65 % did not have (Farrell, Greig & Yu 2019; administrative data, grey literature).
   - Gig workers preferred a plain tracker to finance apps and called taxes "a nightmare" (Hsieh et al. 2025; a qualitative field study).
   - **Rule**: reserves are counted in weeks of the person's own spending; with no income expected, the page says how long the money lasts; a work budget may set aside a share of each payment received for taxes as it arrives, an earmark (finding 41). Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §2.7.

45. **A payment is told several times, and matching its records is an old method.**
   - Record linkage compares two records field by field, weighs each agreement, and sorts pairs into matches, possible matches and non-matches (Fellegi & Sunter 1969; theory); for names, a token similarity built on Jaro–Winkler did best (Cohen, Ravikumar & Fienberg 2003; benchmarks).
   - Budgeting apps match a bank line on its amount, then a window of days, then the payee: the same amount within 10 days (YNAB), within 7 days with the payee first (Actual Budget), a score with three zones (GnuCash) (vendor documentation and source code). A card hold can change its name and amount before it posts (Plaid; vendor documentation), and a SEPA direct debit carries its mandate's reference (Banque de France).
   - That a link needs no one's approval, and is undone when wrong, is Sioul's own inference from finding 11.
   - **Rule**: a movement keeps every source that tells of it: exact references first (the bank's id, the SEPA mandate, an invoice's reference), then a score of amount, days apart and name; one to one; sure, probable or apart, a probable link shown with "Not the same" beside it; no queue to review. Detail: [research/budgets.md](research/budgets.md), §5.

46. **People keep several agendas, one per relationship.**
   - Most people who shared calendars split their events across several, to show each person what concerns them: 20 of 30 adult Google Calendar users did (Thayer et al. 2012; interviews). In 44 families, a family calendar stood beside personal work calendars, which held family events out of the family's reach (Neustaedter, Brush & Greenberg 2009; interviews).
   - Calendar apps let people choose which calendars show at once: sets switched by hand, by the hour, by place or by the system's focus mode (Fantastical), filters over one view of every calendar (TimeTree) (vendor documentation).
   - **Rule**: each agenda says whose it is (yours, a child's, another parent's, a partner's, shared), what it is for (work, admin, leisure) and what you may do with it (read and write, read only, availability only), proposed by Sioul from what it sees and corrected on the agenda; one filter for every view, and named sets switched by hand or by Sioul's own times, never by place, which would need tracking; a work agenda follows the work hours (finding 21). Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.3 and §3.

47. **What matters is one person needed in two places.**
   - Partners argued over clashes neither had looked for ("You didn't look at my calendar." "I don't look two months ahead.": Thayer et al. 2012). Family plans broke at the deviations from routine, about 90 % of what families wrote on their calendars, when the information they needed was missing (Davidoff, Zimmerman & Dey 2010; six families interviewed nightly for six months). None of thirteen co-parenting, family and general calendar apps reviewed warns of overlaps between agendas (vendor documentation).
   - Busy time follows CalDAV's own rule: an opaque event not cancelled, a tentative one tentatively, nothing from a calendar marked transparent (RFC 4791; RFC 6638); Microsoft and Google make all-day events free (vendor documentation).
   - That a conflict follows people rather than pairs of agendas is Sioul's own inference from these findings.
   - **Rule**: a conflict is two events that need the same person at once: their own agendas' events (the work agenda's at any hour), the child events they take and the invitations they accepted; a child's event nobody takes is a question, not a conflict, and an availability agenda conflicts with nothing; the pairs of agendas in the settings hold the exceptions; when only margins overlap, the pair is "tight"; a conflict shows side by side with a thin bracket and one sentence, never as a list, a count or a colour, and is said once only when someone else's change made it, at the next admin window (findings 2 and 21). Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §5.

48. **The mental load is the noticing, the checking and the reminding.**
   - Household cognitive labour is anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring: women did more of it in 26 of 32 different-sex couples, far more of the anticipating and monitoring, while deciding was shared more evenly (Daminger 2019; interviews with 35 couples). Mothers counted delegating and instructing as labour in itself (Robertson et al. 2019; focus groups with 25 mothers). Thinking about family matters went with negative emotions for mothers, not fathers (Offer 2014; experience sampling with 693 parents), and feeling solely responsible for running the household went with lower well-being (Ciciolla & Luthar 2019; a survey of 393 mothers). A systematic review of 31 studies found the same pattern (Reich-Stiebert, Froehlich & Voltmer 2023); the load is invisible, without boundaries and without end (Dean, Churchill & Ruppanner 2022; theory).
   - In 41 of 44 families the mother kept the family calendar, and where no one else scheduled, the mother also reminded the others of what concerned them (Neustaedter, Brush & Greenberg 2009).
   - **Rule**: a child's event or task has one holder, and taking it moves everything: its reminders, margins, ride and follow-up go to the holder; the other parent sees it on the child's page with the holder's name, and receives nothing more; the details stay once, in the child's event; Sioul asks who takes it once, and "later" once more; a holder who declines opens it again, said once to whoever proposed it. Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.1 and §6.

49. **Whoever holds a task holds it from noticing to doing.**
   - Fair Play gives each recurring household task one holder for its conception, planning and execution, since handing over the doing alone keeps the other partner the manager (Rodsky 2019; a practitioner method, without trials). Co-parenting apps keep custody days, which say which parent has a child on a given day (2houses, OurFamilyWizard; vendor documentation).
   - **Rule**: a child and a kind of event (health, dentist, school, activity, clothes) can keep a standing holder, proposed for each new event of that kind; when a held event of a kind with a rhythm is done, a task to book the next one goes to the same parent; for a new event, the proposal is the standing holder, else the parent who has the child that day by the custody days in the child's agenda, else none. Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.1 and §6.

50. **Most separated parents run their days in parallel, and some use messages to control.**
   - Co-parenting after a separation was cooperative, conflicted or disengaged, and seldom close (Maccoby & Mnookin 1992; over 1,000 families followed for three years). Parallel parenting is common: children of cooperative co-parents did better on two outcomes of twelve (Amato, Kane & James 2011; 944 families, a representative survey), or on none (Beckmeyer, Coleman & Ganong 2014; 270 parents). Contentious co-parents used mail and texts to withhold information, limit the other's say, or claim a message never came (Ganong et al. 2012; interviews with 49).
   - In AppClose a shared event enters the other parent's calendar only once accepted; TalkingParents records when each event was viewed (vendor documentation).
   - **Rule**: nothing enters another parent's agenda until they accept it, as a question in their Sioul or as an invitation they answer from any calendar; Sioul keeps each act with its date (proposed, taken, declined), never when something was read. Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.2 and §3.1.

51. **Records invite score-keeping.**
   - Relationships Australia Victoria, a partner in the evaluation of nine co-parenting apps by mediators (Smyth et al. 2023), warns that the apps' "expense-tracking and record-keeping functions may encourage petty account-keeping" (Relationships Australia Victoria 2024; practitioner guidance).
   - In three experiments, people who expected an exchange relationship with their partner on a joint task kept track of each one's inputs (88.2 % took a pen of another colour), while those who expected a communal relationship, as between friends, avoided it (12.5 %) (Clark 1984; read in the author's own account).
   - **Rule**: "who does what" lists, per child and week, each item with its holder's name and the day it was taken, or "nobody yet"; no totals per parent, no history of who did more (findings 8 and 23). Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.2.

52. **A shared calendar can become a way to watch someone.**
   - "A shared calendar may set the expectation that co-parents have the right to monitor each other's movements. This can facilitate controlling and/or abusive behaviour" (Relationships Australia Victoria 2024). People keep separate calendars so as not to open their whole lives "to scrutiny" (Thayer et al. 2012), and how formal a custody arrangement is shapes what separated parents tell each other (Markham et al. 2015; grounded theory with 30 parents).
   - **Rule**: someone else's availability shows to you alone, as plain bands in its own lane, without titles even when the share sends them (a switch on that agenda shows them, off at first); Sioul never writes, re-shares or exports it, nor gives it to an agent; it is consulted when choosing who takes an event, never said as a notice. Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.2 and §6.

53. **A bare "busy" can read as secrecy, and what is shown may depend on who looks.**
   - 26 of 30 people found an entry masked as "busy" impersonal or secretive: "'Busy,' I just think, seems suspect" (Thayer et al. 2012). The standard for availability foresees "different periods of available time depending on which user is making the request" (RFC 7953). Google, Outlook and Proton share a calendar as busy times only, Nextcloud event by event, iCloud not at all; Fantastical copies a calendar into another as "Blocked Time" (vendor documentation; Nextcloud's server code).
   - **Rule** (planned, not built yet): "Share my availability…" makes, in one of your CalDAV accounts, a calendar of busy blocks that Sioul derives from the agendas you pick and keeps up to date, for one recipient at a time; "Busy" by default, an optional word per block for a partner; stopped at any time, and the calendar goes. Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §2.3, §3.3 and §4.3.

54. **One event in two agendas is tied by UID on one server, and by address across servers.**
   - `RELATED-TO` names another event by its UID, PARENT making this one subordinate to it, and `URL` gives "a location where a more dynamic rendition" of the event can be found (RFC 5545); a link by UID must stay within one calendar (RFC 9253); a server that schedules sends invitations itself for attendees unless told `SCHEDULE-AGENT=CLIENT` (RFC 6638); an invitation keeps one UID on both sides, and each update from its organiser replaces the attendee's copy (RFC 5546; Nextcloud's issue tracker); a name-based UUID comes out the same each time from the same names (RFC 9562). Nextcloud keeps every line it is given, Google less, and Outlook has no CalDAV ([compatibility.md](compatibility.md)).
   - **Rule**: where Sioul can write the holder's agenda, the holder gets a copy there, tied to the child's event by `RELATED-TO;RELTYPE=PARENT`, `URL` and `X-SIOUL-LINK;LINKREL=latest-version`, with a UID derived from it so it is never made twice, and following its moves and its cancellation; in a work agenda, a health or school copy says "Away" and is confidential; where Sioul cannot write it, an invitation from your own address waits in the Outbox until you send it, its attendee line marked `SCHEDULE-AGENT=CLIENT`; otherwise, who takes an event is never written as an attendee. Not yet tried against Google, Outlook or Nextcloud. Detail: [research/agendas.md](research/agendas.md), §4.
