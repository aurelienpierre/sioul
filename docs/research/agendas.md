# Several agendas: who does what, and what overlaps

**The question**: people hold several agendas: work, personal, one per child shared with the other parent, the other parent's for availability, each partner's for availability. How should Sioul show them together, filter them, find the overlaps that matter, tie a child's appointment to the parent who takes it and track who does what, so that one parent stops carrying the planning and the reminding alone? What do studies of the mental load, of co-parenting after separation and of family calendars say; what do co-parenting and calendar apps do; which iCalendar standards can carry it?

Researched on 4 November 2026; nothing here is built. The family in the examples is fictional: Alex and Sam, separated, with two children, Léa and Tom.

**How evidence is graded**: **meta** (systematic review); **survey** (cross-sectional, correlational); **diary** (experience sampling); **qualitative** (interviews, focus groups); **theory** (a conceptual paper); **field** (a prototype or study in homes); **expert evaluation**; **practitioner** (a method without trials); **vendor** (an app's help pages, not tested here); **standard** (an RFC, or a server's code). "Reading" lines are design inferences, not findings; "(unverified)" marks what was not confirmed at a primary source.

## 1. Summary of findings
- **The studies agree on the problem, mostly from qualitative work.** Mental labour is anticipating, identifying options, deciding and monitoring; mothers do far more of the anticipating and monitoring, fathers join mostly in deciding (Daminger 2019). Mothers count delegating and instructing as labour in itself (Robertson et al. 2019). Thinking about family matters goes with negative feelings for mothers, not fathers (Offer 2014), and feeling solely responsible goes with lower well-being (Ciciolla and Luthar 2019); a systematic review confirms the gendered pattern (Reich-Stiebert et al. 2023). In 41 of 44 families the mother kept the family calendar, and where only she schedules, she also reminds the others (Neustaedter et al.).
- **So assigning only the doing ("can you drive him?") lifts little.** What must move is the whole object: noticing it is due, its details, its reminders and its follow-up (Rodsky's *Fair Play*: conception, planning, execution).
- **Most separated parents run in parallel rather than co-operate** (Maccoby and Mnookin 1992; Amato et al. 2011). Contentious parents use technology to withhold and control (Ganong et al. 2012). Mediators rate popular co-parenting apps "poor" to "fair", calendars being the hardest part, and a shared calendar can invite monitoring of the other parent's movements (Smyth et al. 2023; Relationships Australia Victoria).
- **None of the thirteen apps read moves the anticipation or the reminders to the parent who takes an event, and none detects overlaps between agendas.** OurFamilyWizard records a pick-up and drop-off parent; AppClose lets a shared event enter a calendar only once accepted; TimeTree makes children participants without accounts; Fantastical has calendar sets and mirrors.
- **Standards**: `RELATED-TO;RELTYPE=PARENT` (by UID) and `URL` (by address), with RFC 9253's LINK under Sioul's own name, express "this event stands for that one"; a LINK by UID is not allowed across calendars. ATTENDEE belongs to meetings, and a scheduling server mails every attendee unless told `SCHEDULE-AGENT=CLIENT`; RFC 9073's PARTICIPANT can say "takes part" without inviting. Busy-only sharing exists in Google, Outlook and Proton links, and per event in Nextcloud (CLASS), not in iCloud.
- **Conflicts should follow people, not pairs of calendars**: an overlap matters when one person would have to be in two places.

## 2. The literature

### 2.1 The mental load
- **Daminger 2019: cognitive labour has four stages, and they are not shared alike.** 70 interviews with both partners of 35 middle- and upper-middle-class couples. Cognitive labour is anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring the result. Women did more of it in 26 of the 32 different-sex couples, and far more of the anticipating and monitoring; men joined mostly in deciding, in ways that "tended to maximize the ratio of power exerted to labor completed".
  - Daminger A (2019). The cognitive dimension of household labor. *American Sociological Review* 84(4):609–633. https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122419859007 — qualitative (small, affluent sample). Figures as reported by JSTOR Daily: https://daily.jstor.org/the-gendered-labor-of-noticing-and-anticipating
  - **Reading**: a tool that only lets a parent *decide* ("you take Tuesday's dentist") leaves the anticipating and the monitoring where they were. An assignment must move the reminders and the follow-up too.
- **Robertson et al. 2019: mothers count delegating and instructing as mental labour.** Seven focus groups, 25 mothers of young children. Six forms: planning and strategising; monitoring and anticipating needs; metaparenting; knowing (learning and remembering); managerial thinking (delegating and instructing); self-regulation. Every participant saw herself as her family's primary mental labourer.
  - Robertson LG, Anderson TL, Hall MEL, Kim CL (2019). Mothers and mental labor: a phenomenological focus group study of family-related thinking work. *Psychology of Women Quarterly* 43(2):184–200. https://doi.org/10.1177/0361684319825581 — qualitative.
  - **Reading**: if handing an appointment over means writing a message, re-explaining it and checking it was seen, nothing moved. The details must live once, in the child's event, and travel with it.
- **Offer 2014: thinking about family matters costs mothers, not fathers.** Experience sampling in the 500 Family Study (402 mothers and 291 fathers in dual-earner, highly educated families). Both thought about family matters about as often, but only mothers' family thoughts went with negative emotions and stress.
  - Offer S (2014). The costs of thinking about work and family: mental labor, work–family spillover, and gender inequality among parents in dual-earner families. *Sociological Forum* 29(4):916–936. https://doi.org/10.1111/socf.12126 — diary.
  - **Reading**: once the other parent takes an event, the first should have nothing left to think about: no copy that keeps reminding them, nothing to check.
- **Ciciolla and Luthar 2019: feeling solely responsible goes with distress.** A survey of 393 partnered US mothers, mostly upper middle class. Most said they alone held the household routines and the vigilance over the children's emotions; feeling disproportionately responsible for household management and for the children went with lower well-being and lower satisfaction with the relationship.
  - Ciciolla L, Luthar SS (2019). Invisible household labor and ramifications for adjustment: mothers as captains of households. *Sex Roles* 81:467–486. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-018-1001-x — survey (correlational).
  - **Reading**: what weighs is responsibility. An assignment must transfer it visibly, to both sides ("Sam takes Tom to the dentist"), not leave the first parent its silent guarantor.
- **Dean, Churchill and Ruppanner 2022: the mental load is cognitive plus emotional labour, and it is invisible, boundaryless and enduring.**
  - Dean L, Churchill B, Ruppanner L (2022). The mental load: building a deeper theoretical understanding of how cognitive and emotional labor overload women and mothers. *Community, Work & Family* 25(1):13–29. https://doi.org/10.1080/13668803.2021.2002813 — theory.
  - **Reading**: the three traits name three things software can do: show who holds what, keep each agenda within its hours, and let an object end for the parent who handed it over.
- **Reich-Stiebert, Froehlich and Voltmer 2023: a systematic review confirms the gendered pattern.** 31 articles (PRISMA): women carry most of the mental labour, most of all when not employed or working part time; no definition is agreed yet.
  - Reich-Stiebert N, Froehlich L, Voltmer J-B (2023). Gendered mental labor: a systematic literature review on the cognitive dimension of unpaid work within the household and childcare. *Sex Roles* 88:475–494. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-023-01362-0 — meta (narrative synthesis).
- **Rodsky's *Fair Play*: whoever holds a task holds its conception, planning and execution.** A book and a deck of 100 cards (the dentist, school forms, winter clothes). A card's holder notices it is needed, arranges it and does it; the partners agree a "minimum standard of care", so that the holder is not supervised. Handing over execution alone keeps the other partner the manager.
  - Rodsky E (2019). *Fair Play*. New York: G. P. Putnam's Sons — practitioner, no trial; its terms as summarised by the press and by readers (the cards' wording was not checked).
  - **Reading**: two levels of assignment follow: a *standing* holder for a kind of thing (Sam holds Tom's dentist every time, the next check-up's reminder included), and a *one-off* holder for one occurrence.

### 2.2 Co-parenting after separation
- **Maccoby and Mnookin 1992: most separated parents do not co-operate closely.** A three-year study of over 1,000 divorcing families: where the children lived in both homes, co-parenting was cooperative, conflicted or disengaged (each parent runs their own time, with little contact).
  - Maccoby EE, Mnookin RH (1992). *Dividing the Child: Social and Legal Dilemmas of Custody*. Harvard University Press — longitudinal survey (the share of each pattern was not checked).
- **Amato, Kane and James 2011: parallel parenting is common, and the "good divorce" helps less than thought.** A cluster analysis of 944 families of the US National Survey of Families and Households found cooperative co-parenting, parallel parenting and single parenting. Children of cooperative co-parents had the fewest behaviour problems and the closest ties to their fathers, but did no better on ten other outcomes. Among 270 parents, Beckmeyer et al. found three types of co-parenting and no difference in the children's adjustment between them.
  - Amato PR, Kane JB, James S (2011). Reconsidering the "good divorce". *Family Relations* 60(5):511–524. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2011.00666.x — survey (representative).
  - Beckmeyer JJ, Coleman M, Ganong LH (2014). Postdivorce coparenting typologies and children's adjustment. *Family Relations* 63(4):526–537. https://doi.org/10.1111/fare.12086 — survey.
  - **Reading**: Sioul must work for parents who barely speak. Each parent sees what is theirs in their own agenda, with nothing to negotiate inside an app.
- **Ganong et al. 2012: cooperative co-parents plan with technology; contentious ones use it to limit and control.** Interviews with 49 divorced co-parents: some withheld information, limited the other's input, or claimed never to have received a message.
  - Ganong LH, Coleman M, Feistman R, Jamison T, Markham MS (2012). Communication technology and postdivorce coparenting. *Family Relations* 61(3):397–409. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2012.00706.x — qualitative.
  - **Reading**: a dated record that an event was offered and taken protects the parent who planned it. It must record acts, not watch people (below).
- **Markham et al. 2015: the formality of the custody arrangement sets what parents tell each other.** Grounded theory with 30 parents sharing physical custody: how formal the arrangement was shaped when, how and what they communicated.
  - Markham MS, Hartenstein JL, Mitchell YT, Aljayyousi-Khalil G (2015; in print 2017). Communication among parents who share physical custody after divorce or separation. *Journal of Family Issues* 38(10):1414–1442. https://doi.org/10.1177/0192513X15616848 — qualitative.
  - **Reading**: what crosses between households is the parents' choice, agenda by agenda. "Availability only" and "full details" must both exist, the narrower by default.
- **Haugen 2010: children in shared residence meet flexibility, ambiguity and rigidity, and want a say.**
  - Haugen GMD (2010). Children's perspectives on everyday experiences of shared residence: time, emotions and agency dilemmas. *Children & Society* 24(2):112–122. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1099-0860.2008.00198.x — qualitative.
  - **Reading**: a child's agenda belongs to the child; an older child may read or write it.
- **Smyth et al. 2023: popular co-parenting apps rate "poor" to "fair", calendars are the hardest part, and shared calendars can serve control.** Fifteen pairs of mediators experienced in high-conflict separations role-played parents in nine apps (2forKids, 2houses, Amicable, AppClose, Divvito, Fayr, OurFamilyWizard, Peaceful Parent, TalkingParents) through five common tasks. All nine rated between "poor" and "fair"; messaging was easy, calendars harder. About half of the users surveyed (49 %) felt an app had reduced conflict. The project's partner Relationships Australia Victoria warns that "a shared calendar may set the expectation that co-parents have the right to monitor each other's movements", which "can facilitate controlling and/or abusive behaviour", and that record-keeping can encourage "petty account-keeping".
  - Smyth BM, Payne JL, Irving M, Heard G (2023). Popular post-separation parenting smartphone apps: an evaluation. *Family Court Review* 61(3):563–585. https://doi.org/10.1111/fcre.12738 — expert evaluation and survey.
  - Relationships Australia Victoria (2024). Smartphone apps for separated co-parents (information sheet). https://relationshipsvictoria.org.au/media/uhgd5yzr/smartphone-apps-for-separated-co-parents-information-sheet_rav_aug-2024.pdf — practitioner. The project: https://polis.cass.anu.edu.au/research/projects/post-separation-parenting-apps (also Irving M (2024), Post-separation parenting apps in the context of family violence: harm minimisation versus harm facilitation, *Law, Technology and Humans* 6(1):59–74; not read).
  - **Reading**: the strongest warning for this design. A parent's own agenda never becomes visible to the other by default; availability is shared by its owner's act, revocably, and never says where the person is; overlaps with the other household stay private to whoever sees them; records concern the children's objects, never a ledger of the other parent.
- **Clark 1984: keeping a tally of each one's part belongs to exchange relationships, not to close ones.** In three experiments, people who expected an exchange relationship with their partner on a joint task kept track of each one's inputs (88.2 % took a pen of another colour), while those who expected a communal relationship avoided it (12.5 %).
  - Clark MS (1984). Record keeping in two types of relationships. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 47(3):549–557. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.47.3.549 — experiment (students). Read in the author's own account: Clark MS (1985). Implications of relationship type for understanding compatibility. In Ickes W (ed.), *Compatible and Incompatible Relationships*, 119–140. Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-5044-9_6
  - **Reading**: a total per parent turns shared care into an exchange. The page of who does what names each item's holder and keeps no tally.

### 2.3 Family calendars in HCI
- **Neustaedter, Brush and Greenberg: in 41 of 44 families the mother kept the family calendar, and in "monocentric" families she also reminds the others.** Interviews and calendar analysis in 44 families. They kept several calendars: a paper family calendar in a central place in 35; personal work calendars in 22, holding family events out of the family's reach. In monocentric families only the primary scheduler schedules and tells the others; others take part more in pericentric (27 %) and polycentric (34 %) families. One father does not check the family calendar because his wife "will let [him] know if there are activities that he needs to be responsible for".
  - Neustaedter C, Brush AJB, Greenberg S (2009). The calendar is crucial: coordination and awareness through the family calendar. *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction* 16(1):1–48. https://doi.org/10.1145/1502800.1502806 — qualitative. Figures from the 2006 report of the same study: https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2006-CalendarCrucial.Report2006-839-32.pdf
  - Neustaedter C, Brush AJB (2006). "LINC-ing" the family: the participatory design of an inkable family calendar. *CHI 2006*, 141–150. https://doi.org/10.1145/1124772.1124796 — participatory design.
  - **Reading**: reminding the father is the observed default. The parent who holds an object must get its reminders from Sioul, so that the primary scheduler stops being the reminder service.
- **Thayer et al. 2012: people split their lives across shared calendars, find "busy" suspect, and argue over unseen overlaps.** Interviews with 30 adult Google Calendar users without children. 20 partitioned their events across several shared calendars, to show each person what concerns them; 26 found masking events as "busy" impersonal or secretive ("'Busy,' I just think, seems suspect"). A couple fought over a clash: "You didn't look at my calendar." "I don't look two months ahead."
  - Thayer A, Bietz MJ, Derthick K, Lee CP (2012). I love you, let's share calendars: calendar sharing as relationship work. *CSCW 2012*, 749–758. https://doi.org/10.1145/2145204.2145317 — qualitative.
  - **Reading**: several agendas per relationship are the norm, not an edge case; a bare "busy" can hurt between partners; an overlap must be shown to the person it concerns, not left for them to find.
- **Davidoff et al. 2010–2011: family plans break at the deviations, at pick-ups and drop-offs.** Six months of nightly interviews with six families: about 40 % of events unfolded as routine, about 90 % of calendar entries concerned deviations, and at those moments families lacked the information they needed.
  - Davidoff S, Zimmerman J, Dey AK (2010). How routine learners can support family coordination. *CHI 2010*, 2461–2470. https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753699 — field.
  - Davidoff S, Ziebart BD, Zimmerman J, Dey AK (2011). Learning patterns of pick-ups and drop-offs to support busy family coordination. *CHI 2011*, 1175–1184. https://doi.org/10.1145/1978942.1979119 — field.
  - **Reading**: "who drives?" matters most when something changes: a change to a child's event must reach the holder's copy at once and be said to the holder once. Learning routines from location, as these prototypes did, needs tracking that Sioul refuses.
- **Plaisant et al. 2006: family calendars should be symmetric.** Older relatives wanted to see the young's activities at least as much as the reverse.
  - Plaisant C, Clamage A, Hutchinson HB, Bederson BB, Druin A (2006). Shared family calendars: promoting symmetry and accessibility. *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction* 13(3):313–346. https://doi.org/10.1145/1183456.1183458 — field.
  - **Reading**: Sioul cannot enforce symmetry between two servers, but it can make each share explicit and say what the other side sees.

## 3. The apps
Read in the vendors' help pages on 4 November 2026 (vendor evidence, not tested). AppClose's and TalkingParents' help centres refuse automated reading; their entries rest on search extracts of those pages.

### 3.1 Co-parenting apps
- **OurFamilyWizard.** An event has an optional "Event For" (one parent), a "Pick-Up and Drop-Off Parent" ("the start and end times for the event will be color-coded to indicate which parent is responsible"), the children concerned and a reminder "for yourself". Events are shared by default, with a "Private" box. Only the author edits or deletes an event, and only before it starts: past events are a "historical record". Parenting time is a separate colour-coded schedule, changed by trade requests, with a record of every change; step-parents or grandparents can be given view access. Export is an `.ics` file. No conflict detection is described.
  - https://support.ourfamilywizard.com/hc/en-us/articles/42667018503437-How-do-I-add-an-event-to-the-calendar ; https://www.ourfamilywizard.com/blog/sharing-calendars-what-can-my-co-parent-see
- **2houses.** A custody schedule made "by selecting days and parents", a holiday schedule that "will overlay the standard schedule", colours per parent, one-off and repeating events. A change request names a parent and a date; until it is accepted, "both of the schedules are visible". No per-event assignment or conflict detection is described.
  - https://www.2houses.com/en/blog/first-steps-on-2houses
- **AppClose.** A parenting calendar holds the custody schedule and "swap days" requests, pending until approved or declined. Events on other calendars can be shared with a co-parent or a third party, who approves or declines them and adds them to one of their own calendars. "AppClose Solo" sends events and requests by text or mail to a parent without the app. No conflict detection is described.
  - https://support.appclose.com/hc/en-us/articles/30836914343451-How-do-I-create-an-event ; https://support.appclose.com/hc/en-us/articles/27579820894491-How-do-I-create-a-swap-days-request ; https://blog.appclose.com/why-use-appclose.html
- **TalkingParents.** One shared calendar: both parents "view all shared events, but only edit what you created". Its record shows "when an event was viewed, created, and edited"; shared events can be copied to a personal Google calendar. No assignment, private event or conflict detection is described.
  - https://talkingparents.com/features/shared-calendar

### 3.2 Family calendar apps
- **Cozi.** Up to 12 members, each with a colour; an event names who is involved and who gets its reminders; to-do lists; outside calendars added as subscribed ICS feeds. https://www.cozi.com/?p=4725 ; https://cozi.com/calendar
- **TimeTree.** Sharing is set per calendar ("events are shared only among members who are participating in the same calendar"). Since 2024, account-free "virtual members" (children, pets, cars) can be event participants; since January 2026 a Home Calendar shows every calendar, external ones included, with filters. https://support.timetreeapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/900002172746-Can-other-calendar-members-see-my-events ; https://timetreeapp.com/intl/en/newsroom/2024-07-16/veirtual-member-released ; https://timetreeapp.com/intl/en/newsroom/2026-01-13/renewal-timetree-newsroom
- **FamCal.** One family group, a colour per member ("view or add events by person"), task lists that "notify a specific member or the whole family", two-way sync with the device's calendar. https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1098999871

### 3.3 General calendars
- **Google Calendar.** Sharing levels: "See only free/busy (hide details)", "See all event details", "Make changes to events", "Make changes and manage sharing"; editors see a private event as a busy block, and a private event marked free not at all. Events carry `visibility` (private, with "confidential" kept "for compatibility reasons") and `transparency`. Google's model has no relation between events: only `extendedProperties` and `source` (an http(s) URL that "can only be seen or modified by the creator of the event") can hold a link. A family group comes with a shared "Family" calendar (third-party descriptions, not read at Google). https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/37082 ; https://developers.google.com/workspace/calendar/api/v3/reference/events
- **Apple.** Family Sharing adds a "Family" calendar and reminder list. An iCloud calendar is shared view-only or view-and-edit with iCloud users, or published read-only; there is no busy-only share. Reminders in a shared list can be assigned to one person, and gather in "Assigned to Me". https://support.apple.com/en-us/105124 ; https://support.apple.com/guide/calendar/icl32362
- **Microsoft Outlook.** "Can view when I'm busy", "Can view titles and locations", "Can view all details", "Can edit", "Delegate"; published HTML and ICS links are read-only. Outlook has no CalDAV: Sioul reaches it only through invitations and published links. https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/outlook/share-your-calendar-in-outlook-on-the-web
- **Fantastical.** Calendar sets group calendars, switched by hand, by time, by place or by the system's Focus, each with its default calendar and task list. Calendar mirroring (June 2026) copies a source calendar's events into another as "Blocked Time", or with their titles, keeping times, recurrence and "free/busy status"; one rule per pair, no chaining, and the destination must "support custom properties", by which Fantastical knows its copies. https://flexibits.com/fantastical-ios/help/calendar-sets ; https://flexibits.com/fantastical/help/calendar-mirroring
- **Nextcloud.** Calendars are shared with users or groups, read-only or editable, or published as read-only links; there is no busy-only share of a whole calendar (issue #1044, closed in 2019 by pointing at free/busy between users of one server). Per event, the server's code decides: `CLASS:PRIVATE` events are left out for everyone but the owner (`Calendar::getChildren`), and `CLASS:CONFIDENTIAL` events reach read-only sharees and links as "Busy", stripped of all but their times, recurrence and UID (`CalendarObject::createConfidentialObject`); read-only sharees also lose the alarms. The calendar app calls these "When shared show full event / show only busy / hide this event". A sharee with edit rights sees confidential events in full (private ones stay hidden from every sharee). Each calendar keeps `schedule-calendar-transp`. https://github.com/nextcloud/server/tree/master/apps/dav/lib/CalDAV ; https://github.com/nextcloud/calendar/issues/1044
- **Proton Calendar.** Shared with Proton users ("View" or "Edit"), or by link: "Limited view" ("shows when you're busy but doesn't show any event details") or "Full view". When inviting, participants' busy slots show, inside one organisation, for those who share them. https://proton.me/support/share-calendar-via-link ; https://proton.me/support/calendar-availability

**What it shows.** (1) The co-parenting apps assign at most a driver; none moves the anticipation or the follow-up, and none sends the next occurrence's reminder to the parent who holds it. (2) None detects overlaps between agendas; the general calendars look at free/busy only when inviting. (3) Each co-parenting app is a silo beside the parents' real agendas, joined at best by an `.ics` export or a one-way copy. (4) Busy-only sharing exists in Google, Outlook and Proton links, and per event in Nextcloud; not in iCloud. (5) Worth borrowing: AppClose's consent, OurFamilyWizard's record and driver, TimeTree's children as participants, Fantastical's sets and mirrors.

## 4. The standards
Read in the RFC texts and in Nextcloud's server code on 4 November 2026.

### 4.1 What each property says
- **RELATED-TO** (RFC 5545 §3.8.4.5, extended by RFC 9253 §9.1) holds another component's UID. Its default type, PARENT, "indicates that the calendar component is a subordinate of the referenced calendar component"; CHILD and SIBLING are the others. RFC 9253 adds temporal types, FIRST, NEXT, DEPENDS-ON, REFID and CONCEPT, and URI values for them, but "the value type MUST be UID when the PARENT, SIBLING, or CHILD relationships are specified". It notes that "if a PARENT calendar component is canceled or deleted, then there is an implied impact to the related CHILD calendar components", while the property "is intended only to provide information". UIDs are global, so the target may sit in another calendar. Sioul already writes `RELATED-TO;RELTYPE=PARENT` for steps and for rides around an appointment, and a phone's ical4j keeps it ([compatibility.md](../compatibility.md)).
- **LINK** (RFC 9253 §8.2) is a typed reference (VALUE=URI, UID or XML-REFERENCE) with a required LINKREL, a registered link relation or a URI; the RFC names `latest-version`, which "identifies the latest version of the event information". A LINK by UID "MUST refer to another component within the same collection"; across collections it must become a URI (§2). Sioul writes LINK as `X-SIOUL-LINK`, since ical4j before 4.1.0 crashes on it.
- **URL** (RFC 5545 §3.8.4.6), once per component: "a location where a more dynamic rendition of the calendar information associated with the calendar component can be found".
- **REFID** (RFC 9253 §8.3) groups components by a text key without meaning; **CONCEPT** (§8.1) is a URI category with meaning. Sioul writes them as `X-SIOUL-REFID` (projects) and `X-SIOUL-CONCEPT` (kinds).
- **ATTENDEE and ORGANIZER** (RFC 5545 §3.8.4.1, §3.8.4.3) belong to group-scheduled components: ATTENDEE "MUST NOT be specified" when publishing, and "is not specified" in components "only on a single user's calendar". ROLE is CHAIR, REQ-PARTICIPANT, OPT-PARTICIPANT or NON-PARTICIPANT ("copied for information purposes only"), with PARTSTAT, RSVP and delegation, by which an attendee "grants another CU … the right to attend on their behalf" (RFC 5546 §2.1.2). The tasks draft calls an assignee "equivalent to an 'Attendee' of a task" and adds the statuses IN-PROCESS and FAILED (draft-ietf-calext-ical-tasks-17, work in progress).
- **PARTICIPANT** (RFC 9073 §7) describes someone involved, with PARTICIPANT-TYPE (ACTIVE, INACTIVE, CONTACT, PLANNER-CONTACT…). It "may also represent someone or something that is NOT to receive scheduling messages": it is schedulable only when its CALENDAR-ADDRESS equals an ATTENDEE's. It is the standard way to say "Sam takes part" without inviting anyone. No client supporting it was found, and ical4j on phones was not checked.
- **TRANSP** (RFC 5545 §3.8.2.7): events that take a person's time "SHOULD be recorded as OPAQUE" (the default); TRANSPARENT ones are "invisible to free/busy time searches".
- **CLASS** (RFC 5545 §3.8.1.3): PUBLIC (default), PRIVATE, CONFIDENTIAL. The RFC leaves their meaning to each system: they "cannot serve as an enforcement statement for a system receiving an iCalendar object" and record "the intention of the calendar owner". Nextcloud's reading is the practical one (§3.3); Google reads private, and confidential, as "only event attendees may view event details".

### 4.2 Invitations: iTIP, iMIP and CalDAV scheduling
- **iTIP** (RFC 5546) defines PUBLISH, REQUEST, REPLY, ADD, CANCEL, REFRESH, COUNTER and DECLINECOUNTER. The attendee's copy keeps the organizer's UID; an update is a new REQUEST with a higher SEQUENCE; a reply carries the attendee's PARTSTAT. **iMIP** (RFC 6047) carries them by mail. Sioul reads REQUEST, CANCEL and PUBLISH and answers them, but never writes a REQUEST.
- **CalDAV scheduling** (RFC 6638) is implicit: a server that implements it sends invitations itself when an event with ATTENDEE lines is stored in the organizer's calendar, unless the line carries `SCHEDULE-AGENT=CLIENT` ("the client is handling scheduling messages") or `NONE`; unknown values count as NONE. The collection property `schedule-calendar-transp` says whether a whole calendar "ought to contribute to the owner's busy time".
- **Reading**: an invitation is the only standard tie that crosses servers and keeps one UID on both sides, and its REPLY is a dated record ("Sam accepted"). Its costs: the other parent's address goes into the child's event; on a scheduling server, an ATTENDEE line Sioul writes makes the server mail Sam unless marked `SCHEDULE-AGENT=CLIENT`; and each update from the organizer replaces the attendee's copy, dropping the attendee's own lines (Nextcloud calendar issue #7764, open). A stand-in reached by invitation can hold nothing Sioul needs on the attendee's side.

### 4.3 Availability
- **VFREEBUSY** (RFC 5545 §3.6.4) lists busy periods (FBTYPE BUSY, BUSY-TENTATIVE, BUSY-UNAVAILABLE). CalDAV's free-busy-query (RFC 4791 §7.10) computes it from OPAQUE events only: CANCELLED is free, TENTATIVE is BUSY-TENTATIVE. Its `read-free-busy` privilege (§6.1.1) is the standard busy-only share: servers "MUST allow the CALDAV:read-free-busy to be granted without the DAV:read privilege".
- **VAVAILABILITY** (RFC 7953) states repeating availability: AVAILABLE components inside a VAVAILABILITY, whose other time counts as BUSY-UNAVAILABLE in free/busy lookups. Its first example is "normal 'office hours'", and it adds that "it ought be possible to present different periods of available time depending on which user is making the request". Nextcloud's weekly availability component converts its slots to and from VAVAILABILITY (https://npmjs.com/package/@nextcloud/calendar-availability-vue; where the server keeps it was not checked).
- **vCard FBURL** (RFC 6350 §6.9.1) puts "the URI for the busy time" of a person in their contact card.
- How a Google free/busy share, or an Outlook busy-only ICS, looks to a CalDAV or ICS reader was not checked.

### 4.4 "This event in my agenda stands for that event in the child's agenda"
- **By UID, inside Sioul**: `RELATED-TO;RELTYPE=PARENT:<UID of the child's event>`. PARENT is the right type: the stand-in exists because of the child's event, follows it, and goes when it is cancelled, the implied impact RFC 9253 names. SIBLING would make them peers and lose the direction "the details are there"; `LINK;VALUE=UID` is not allowed across calendars. Sioul's own address for it, `sioul:event/<UID>`, then resolves as for any event.
- **By URL, outside**: `URL:<address of the child's event>` (RFC 5545), and the typed form `X-SIOUL-LINK;LINKREL=latest-version;VALUE=URI:<the same address>` (RFC 9253's LINK under Sioul's name), which tells a stand-in from a ride (rides carry `X-SIOUL-ANCHOR`) and survives a client that rewrites URL. The address is the event's CalDAV URL, which either parent opens with their access to the child's calendar.
- **What other clients show**: no client read shows RELATED-TO or LINK on an event, and Sioul's compatibility notes found none showing RFC 9253's lines. URL is shown by some desktop clients (Apple Calendar, Thunderbird: unverified). DESCRIPTION is shown everywhere, so the stand-in's description should end with one line saying where the details are.
- **What survives a round trip**: Nextcloud and Baïkal (sabre/dav) store what they are given, and DAVx⁵ keeps unknown lines on events ([compatibility.md](../compatibility.md)). Google keeps less than it is given and has no relation of its own; Sioul writes no rides into Google calendars for this reason, and should not count on these lines there. Microsoft offers no CalDAV: only an invitation reaches it, and only the iTIP core (UID, ORGANIZER, ATTENDEE, PARTSTAT, SEQUENCE) can be counted on. iCloud was not checked. Through any invitation, the organizer's updates rewrite the attendee's copy. Hence a rule that needs no server's goodwill: **derive the stand-in's UID from the child's event UID and the target calendar** (a name-based UUID, version 5 of RFC 9562), so that Sioul finds an existing copy, and never makes a second one, even where every other line was dropped.

## 5. Conflicts

### 5.1 What counts
- **An event takes time** when it is OPAQUE (no TRANSP, or OPAQUE), not CANCELLED and not declined by the person; TENTATIVE takes time tentatively. This is CalDAV's own table for busy time (RFC 4791 §7.10). A calendar marked transparent as a whole (`schedule-calendar-transp`) takes none.
- **All-day events**: by the RFC, an all-day event without TRANSP is opaque, but the large calendars make them free. Microsoft says "the block of time for an all day event is typically shown as free" (https://support.microsoft.com/ru-RU/Outlook/create-an-all-day-event, via a search extract), and Google offers no setting to make them busy by default (a community answer). Sioul should honour TRANSP when it is written and treat an all-day event without it as free.
- **Margins**: Sioul keeps time before and after events (`X-SIOUL-BEFORE`, `X-SIOUL-AFTER`) and rides around appointments. When only margins overlap, the pair is *tight*, not *at the same time*, and is said so.

### 5.2 Between which agendas
What matters is whether **one person would have to be in two places**. A pair of agendas is the wrong unit: two children's appointments at the same hour conflict only for a parent who takes both, and not at all if each parent takes one. So:
- For each person, the events that need them are those of their own agendas, the child events they hold, and the invitations they accepted. Overlaps among these are conflicts.
- A child's event that nobody holds yet is nobody's conflict. It is a question ("who takes it?"), and that is where availability helps ("Sam is busy then").
- Availability-only agendas (the other household, partners) conflict with nothing by default; they are consulted when choosing who takes an event, or a time.
- The triangular matrix (each pair of agendas checked or not, the diagonal meaning double bookings within one agenda) is the place for exceptions, **prefilled from these rules**: for example, checking Léa's agenda against Tom's for events nobody holds yet, because one car serves both.

Evidence: partners fought over overlaps neither had looked for (Thayer et al. 2012); plans break at the deviations (Davidoff et al. 2010); none of the thirteen apps detects overlaps between agendas (§3.4).

### 5.3 Showing it calmly
- In the timeline, overlapping events stand side by side, as in every calendar, and a thin neutral bracket joins a conflicting pair. The event's card says it in one sentence: "At the same time as Léa's swimming, which you take." No badge, no red, no count, no list of conflicts.
- Sioul does not notify a conflict the person comes across while browsing. When someone else's change creates one (Sam moves the dentist onto Alex's meeting), Sioul says it **once**, at the next admin window, never in the evening for work matters ([areas.md](../areas.md)), with three answers: ask Sam to take it (a draft the person sends), move my event (if Sioul may write it), keep both.

## 6. Recommendations for Sioul
Each names its evidence. None is built.

### The agendas
1. **Give each calendar a role and a person, proposed by Sioul and corrected once.** Roles: mine (with its area: work, admin, leisure), a child's (shared), another household's (availability), a partner's (availability). Sioul proposes them from what it sees (read-only and owned by someone else: availability; shared and writable with another adult: a child's) and keeps them in its settings, not in the calendars. People already split their lives across calendars per relationship (Thayer et al., §2.3), and families keep several (Neustaedter et al.).
2. **The work agenda follows the hours; its busy time does not.** Its events and reminders never reach the person outside work hours, as for mail ([areas.md](../areas.md)), but its busy time still counts against the children's events, since that is where conflicts arise. The personal agenda is set to reach "all day" or "outside work hours".

### The data model
3. **The child's event holds the details, once**: notes, what to bring, place, contacts. Instructing the other parent is itself labour (Robertson et al., §2.1).
4. **Write who takes it in the child's event or task**: `X-SIOUL-TAKEN-BY;CN=Sam;PARTSTAT=ACCEPTED;SINCE=20261103T181500Z:mailto:sam@example.org`, with `PARTSTAT=NEEDS-ACTION` while proposed (iTIP's words). Add a second line with `PART=BACK` when one parent drops off and the other picks up (OurFamilyWizard's driver field, §3.1, split in two). A repeating lesson takes its holder for the series, and a changed occurrence (RECURRENCE-ID) carries its own. Not ATTENDEE: RFC 5545 keeps it for meetings, and a scheduling server would mail the person (§4.2). RFC 9073's PARTICIPANT (type ACTIVE, without ATTENDEE) is the standard equivalent, to write beside it once a phone's ical4j is shown to keep it.
5. **Make the stand-in in the holder's own agenda** when Sioul can write it: title, time and place copied; the holder's margins and ride added; `RELATED-TO;RELTYPE=PARENT:<child event UID>`, `URL:<child event address>` and `X-SIOUL-LINK;LINKREL=latest-version;VALUE=URI:<the same>`; a UID derived from the child event's UID and the target calendar; a description ending "Details: Tom's agenda", with the address; `CLASS:CONFIDENTIAL` for health and school kinds (§4.4). A move, a cancellation or a new holder follows the child's event, as rides follow their appointment now (`X-SIOUL-UNCONFIRMED`, `X-SIOUL-CANCELLED-WITH`), each said once, with Undo. In a Google calendar the tie lives in the derived UID and Sioul's own index, not in the lines.
6. **Keep kinds and standing holders.** The kind of a child's event (health, dentist, school, activity, clothes) is an `X-SIOUL-CONCEPT` tag URI, as for tasks. A standing holder per child and kind ("Sam holds Tom's dentist") lives in Sioul's settings and is proposed for each new event of that kind; the next anticipation (the check-up in six months) becomes a task in the child's list, held by the same parent. This is *Fair Play*'s card (§2.1), and it moves Daminger's anticipating and monitoring, not only the deciding.
7. **Tasks** (a winter coat) are VTODOs in the child's shared task list, with the same holder line. They sit in the holder's Next and plan; the other parent sees them on the child's page with the holder's name, never in their own plan.

### Assignment and who does what
8. **Assigning moves everything**: reminders, margins, the ride and the follow-up task go to the holder, and the other parent receives nothing more about it. Thinking about family matters is what costs mothers (Offer, §2.1), feeling solely responsible is what weighs (Ciciolla and Luthar), and in monocentric families the primary scheduler reminds everyone (Neustaedter et al., §2.3). If the holder declines, the event is open again for both, said once to whoever proposed it.
9. **Ask once, and propose a default.** When an event enters a child's agenda without a holder, Sioul asks its author once: "Who takes it? Me, Sam, later." "Later" brings the question back once, at the next admin window, and never again. The default proposed is the standing holder; else the parent who has the child that day, when the child's agenda holds a custody schedule (all-day "With Sam" days, as 2houses and OurFamilyWizard keep them, §3.1); else none.
10. **Ask for consent both ways, and record acts only.** A proposal reaches the other parent as one quiet question in their Sioul ("Alex proposes that you take Tom to the dentist on Tuesday at 10:00: take it, or not this time"), or as an invitation (rec. 13). It enters their agenda only when accepted (AppClose's model, §3.1; Sioul's first rule). Sioul keeps the act ("Sam took it on 3 November"), which answers "I never got it" (Ganong et al., §2.2), and never the moment something was read (TalkingParents' "viewed" times), which serves monitoring (§2.2).
11. **Add a "who does what" page per child and per week**, each item with its holder's name, and a filter "what I take" across children. Show no totals per parent and no history of who did more: records invite "petty account-keeping" (§2.2), a tally of each one's part belongs to exchange relationships rather than close ones (Clark 1984, §2.2), and Sioul shows no counts.

### Invitations or linked events
12. **Use a linked event when Sioul can write the holder's agenda** (both agendas in this Sioul, or a holder whose own Sioul reads the child's calendar). No mail is sent: each Sioul makes the stand-in in its own person's agenda from the holder line.
13. **Use an invitation when it cannot** (the other parent's own Google or Outlook calendar). Sioul offers an iTIP REQUEST by mail from the person's own address, keeping the child's event UID: ORGANIZER the sender, ATTENDEE the holder with `ROLE=REQ-PARTICIPANT;RSVP=TRUE`, the details needed in DESCRIPTION, and URL back to the child's event. On a scheduling server the stored ATTENDEE line carries `SCHEDULE-AGENT=CLIENT`, so the server sends no second invitation. The REPLY sets the holder line's PARTSTAT and date. Updates and cancellations leave only when the person presses Send, from the Outbox. This is Sioul's first writer of `METHOD:REQUEST`: test it against Google, Outlook and Nextcloud before use.

### What stays private
14. **Show availability agendas as busy time only, and only to their reader.** Sioul stores what the share gives and nothing more; shows it as plain bands in its own lane, without titles even when the share has them, unless the person asks; never writes, re-shares or exports it; and keeps it from agents (MCP). It is consulted when choosing who takes an event or when to meet, never as a standing overlay or a notice ("Sam is away all week" is not Sioul's to say). A shared calendar "may set the expectation that co-parents have the right to monitor each other's movements" (§2.2).
15. **Offer to share one's own availability**, per recipient, as a read-only calendar of busy blocks that Sioul derives from the agendas the person picks (Fantastical's "Blocked Time" mirrors, §3.3). It works on every server and can differ by recipient, as RFC 7953 wishes (§4.3). "Busy" by default for a co-parent; an optional word per block for a partner, since partners read a bare "busy" as secrecy (26 of 30, Thayer et al.). Revocable at any time.
16. **Keep a child's health in the child's agenda.** A stand-in in a work agenda carries no title by default ("Away") and is CONFIDENTIAL; in the personal agenda it carries its title.

### The views
17. **Put filters in every view**: a row of agenda chips (name and colour, no numbers) above the Agenda, the timeline, Next and the Gantt chart, and named sets ("Tom and Léa", "My week") switched by hand or by Sioul's own times (work, admin, leisure), not by location, which would need tracking (Fantastical's sets and TimeTree's filters, §3).
18. **Show lanes, and conflicts per person**: the timeline shows one lane per agenda or per person, so overlaps read as positions; availability lanes are bands; conflicts follow people, not pairs of calendars, and are shown and said as §5 describes, with the triangular matrix in settings for exceptions.
