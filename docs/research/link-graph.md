# The chain of ties: relevance from one thing, and how to show it

**The question**: every thing in Sioul is tied to others (a task made from a message, a payment told by a mail, a note in a project, a letter for a child), and those ties are written in many places. From any one thing, how should Sioul show the chain of ties: what it is tied to, what those are tied to, both ways, whoever wrote the tie; which things to keep and which to leave out; how to tell their kinds apart without colour alone; and how to draw it so that it reads, on a computer and on a phone, with a screen reader too?

Researched on 5 November 2026; built the same day ([tasks.md](../tasks.md#the-chain-of-ties); findings 67 to 69 of [research.md](../research.md)). The guide's page is [Ties](../../website/docs/guide/ties.md).

**How evidence is graded**: **trial** (a controlled experiment with people), **field study**, **survey**, **qualitative**, **benchmark** (an algorithm measured on data sets, no people), **theory**, **vendor documentation**, **standard**, and **own inference** (a design reading, marked so).

## Summary of findings
- **Every tie Sioul knows can be read from both ends in memory**, built in one pass over the files that already hold them; a second store of copies is what sank NEPOMUK (§1, §3.1).
- **People re-find by small steps from what they know**; a small graph helps to follow a chain, a list serves looking something up (§3.1, §3.3, §3.4).
- **Relevance from one thing is interest that falls with each step**, weighed by the kind of tie, with hubs as places to stop and count rather than go through (§2).
- **Seventeen kinds are too many for colour alone**: a shape per family, an icon per kind and the kind in words, each enough alone (§4).
- **A layered tree read from the left** keeps the chain as a sentence and stays still when more is shown (§5).

## 1. Where ties are written, and how the index reads them

Each tie stays written where it is (a task's `LINK`, `RELATED-TO`, `CONTACT` and `REFID`; an event's guests and `X-SIOUL-TAKEN-BY`; a note's links and front matter; a draft's links; a budget line's links; `links.toml`; the money store; the persons, papers, letters and contracts; a project's routes and files; a message's headers). Sioul builds one index from them in memory (`ties::TieIndex`): each address once in one table, each tie two small numbers into it with its kind, the reverse entry made with the forward one. It is never written to a file, a cache, the sharing or a log, since it gathers the ties of sealed stores with the plain ones.

| Tie | Held by | Read as |
|---|---|---|
| Task → message, note, draft, event, web page | the task (`LINK`) | made from, its notes, its appointment, linked |
| Task → task (a step, a wait) | the step; the one that waits | a step of, waits for |
| Task, event → contact; event → guest | the task (`CONTACT`), the event (`ATTENDEE`, `ORGANIZER`) | who |
| Task, event, note, letter, invoice → project | `REFID`, a tie, the project's files, the letter's and invoice's own | in the project |
| Event → event (a holder's stand-in) | the stand-in (`RELATED-TO;RELTYPE=PARENT`) | stands for |
| Event → person (a child's agenda, who takes it) | the agenda's role, `X-SIOUL-TAKEN-BY` | in the agenda of, taken by |
| Note → anything | the note | mentions |
| Draft → the message it answers | the draft | answers |
| Message → message | the later one (`In-Reply-To`, `References`) | answers; the same conversation |
| Message → contact | its sender's address | from |
| Message → project | the project's routes | in the project (by its routes) |
| Payment → its line, mail, letter, invoice, contract, budget | the payment as the budgets page derives it, the money store | told by, its contract, its budget |
| Payment → payment | the money store (`together`) | together with |
| Paper, letter → person; site → person | `links.toml` (`holder`, `to`, `for`) | for, opened for |
| Paper → its renewal task; letter → its task | the paper, the letter | made from |
| Contract → paper; contract, invoice, project → a card by name | the contract, the invoice, the project | its paper, with, client |
| Person → card; a child's other parent | the persons' store | their card, parent of |
| Time noted → its task | the time log | a count on the task, never a thing of its own |

- **A tie computed from a message's headers** (a reply, a conversation, its sender, a project's routes, the ties Sioul makes as mail arrives) is followed only through mail the Porch admits, as a project's page and the agents' consent take it: a forged message citing two conversations never joins them. A tie made by hand is followed as written.
- **A payment is derived**, not stored: the index takes the payments the budgets page derives, gives each its one address, and maps every other key a payment goes by (its line's, its occurrence's, its bank line's) to it.
- **Texts and calls are not in it**: they have no address of their own nor a card to open (own inference; a count on the contact may come later).

## 2. Relevance from an entry point

### 2.1 Degree of interest: Furnas (1986), van Ham and Perer (2009)
- **Furnas (1986)**, "Generalized fisheye views", CHI '86, pp. 16–23, doi:10.1145/22627.22342 (theory, with small demonstrations). A thing's degree of interest given a focus is its a-priori importance minus its distance from the focus, `DOI(x | y) = API(x) − D(x, y)`; what falls under a threshold is left out. It was defined on trees, where interest is nested (nothing in a subtree is more interesting than its root), which makes the cut trivial.
- **van Ham and Perer (2009)**, "'Search, show context, expand on demand': supporting large graph exploration with degree-of-interest", *IEEE TVCG* 15(6), pp. 953–960, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2009.108; read in full (author's copy, perer.org). Evidence: a system and case studies by the authors on 300,000 court decisions and 3.3 million citations; **no user study** ("further user testing might lead to more refined DOI functions", §4). What it gives Sioul:
  - the function, `DOI(x | y, z) = α·API_diff(x) + β·UI_diff(x, z) + γ·D(x, y)`, a weighted sum of a-priori interest, the user's search interest and the distance from the focus, each part tunable by a slider;
  - interest diffused over ties, `API_diff(x) = max(API(x), δ · max over neighbours n of API_diff(n) / EI(e, x, n))`, with `δ` in [0, 1) and `EI` a cost per kind of tie ("less interest in following a link"); in their data ties had no kind, so `EI` was constant;
  - **the cut is a budget, not a threshold**: "a connected subgraph F of size at most S that contains y and has maximal total interest", found greedily: start from the focus, repeatedly take the candidate with the highest DOI from a heap, add its neighbours as candidates, stop at S nodes; O(S log S);
  - **expand on demand**: a click on a node adds its top N hidden neighbours by the same DOI; clicking again adds N more;
  - **what is left out is shown, counted**: "fading edges emanate from nodes to show the user that more connections are available", capped per node, "we also display the precise number on each node";
  - **few suggestions**: the n most interesting directions for expansion highlighted, "n should be small (n < 5)", and "in most cases this top n will be stable", because what is farther from the focus is less interesting;
  - **layout**: force-directed by stress majorization, chosen over gradient descent for "far better layout stability"; after an expansion "a new layout is computed based on the initial layout and the whole graph is then smoothly animated".
- **Where Sioul must differ** (own inference): in court citations a much-cited decision is *more* interesting (their API is in-degree and court level). In a person's admin a hub is the opposite: a project with 500 messages, a correspondent of years, a budget of every movement. Sioul's a-priori term must lower hubs, not raise them (§2.4).

### 2.2 Spreading activation: Crestani (1997)
- **Crestani (1997)**, "Application of spreading activation techniques in information retrieval", *Artificial Intelligence Review* 11(6), pp. 453–482, doi:10.1023/A:1006569829653 (a review of associative retrieval systems; read through its abstract and through secondary descriptions, the full text is behind a paywall and was not read). Activation starts at the query's nodes and flows along ties, weakened at each step, and is bounded by four constraints, which are the vocabulary Sioul needs:
  - a **distance** constraint: stop at nodes many ties away;
  - a **fan-out** constraint: stop, or weaken, at nodes tied to very many others ("highly connected nodes"), the general form of hub damping;
  - a **path** constraint: follow preferred kinds of ties only, as the application's inference rules say;
  - an **activation** threshold: a node passes activation on only above a level.
- Spreading activation is the same computation as van Ham and Perer's diffusion with `max`, read the other way round: interest flows from the focus, weakened by each tie's cost. Its constraints are rules a person can be told in words ("Sioul does not go on through a project's other messages"), which makes it explainable.

### 2.3 Random walk with restart and personalised PageRank
- **Tong, Faloutsos and Pan (2006)**, "Fast random walk with restart and its applications", ICDM 2006, pp. 613–622, doi:10.1109/ICDM.2006.70 (benchmark on image and bibliographic graphs). A walker on the graph returns to the focus with probability `c` at each step; the stationary probability of each node is its relevance to the focus. The exact solution needs a matrix inversion; their methods (low-rank approximation, partitions) cut its cost up to 150 times with over 90 % of the quality.
- **Andersen, Chung and Lang (2006)**, "Local graph partitioning using PageRank vectors", FOCS 2006, doi:10.1109/FOCS.2006.44 (theory; author's full version read). Their `ApproximatePR` computes an ε-approximate personalised PageRank vector by "push" operations that move probability from a residual to the estimate, only at nodes where the residual is at least ε times the degree. **Its running time is O(1/(εα)), independent of the size of the graph**, and it touches only nodes near the seed. They then rank nodes by **probability divided by degree** (`p/d`, the sweep), which removes the walk's pull towards hubs.
- What a walk does well: a node reached by several paths scores higher than one reached by one; a hub passes only 1/degree of its weight to each neighbour, which damps a project's 500 messages by itself.
- What it does badly here (own inference):
  - **explaining**: a stationary probability is not a sentence; "this task is here because it is 0.031" says nothing to the person, and the several paths that make a score cannot be shown at once;
  - **stability**: the scores are a global solution; adding a seed, a tie or a node changes every score a little, so a ranking cut at S nodes can swap nodes at its edge after an unrelated change. "+" must not re-run the walk with the expanded node as a second seed, or the rest of the picture moves.
  - on a phone, the push form is cheap (§6), so cost is not the objection.

### 2.4 Hubs
- Sioul's hubs (own inference from §1): a project (all its mail, tasks, events, notes), a contact (all their mail, texts, calls, events), a person (their agendas' events, papers, letters), a budget (all its movements), a site (its person, its mail by domain), a long conversation (dozens of messages).
- Three remedies in the sources: divide by degree as the walk does (Tong et al. 2006; `p/d` in Andersen et al. 2006); stop or weaken at highly connected nodes (Crestani's fan-out constraint); show the hub and **count** what it holds rather than draw it (van Ham and Perer's numbers on nodes and fading edges).
- **For Sioul, a hub is a place to stop, not a place to go through** (own inference): reaching the project from a payment is the point of a chain; going on from the project to its 499 other messages is noise. The rule that says this in words: *Sioul follows a thing's own ties, and stops at the collections it belongs to (a project, a contact, a person, a budget, a site, a conversation), shown with how many things they hold; "+" opens them.* A collection opened as the entry point is the exception: its members are then its direct ties.

### 2.5 Typed paths: meta-paths, Sun et al. (2011)
- **Sun, Han, Yan, Yu and Wu (2011)**, "PathSim: meta path-based top-k similarity search in heterogeneous information networks", *PVLDB* 4(11), pp. 992–1003, doi:10.14778/3402707.3402736 (benchmark on bibliographic networks). In a graph whose nodes and ties have types, a meta-path is a sequence of types (author → paper → venue → paper → author); each meta-path is a different meaning of "related", and a similarity is computed along a chosen one.
- For Sioul the lesson is the type, not the formula: PathSim measures peers of the same type, while the graph wants the chain from one object to others of any type. What carries over:
  - the **kind of each tie** (Sioul's `how`: made from, answers, a step of, waits for, its appointment, in project, involves, mentions, a source of the movement…) is the strongest signal of relevance, and each kind can be given a weight;
  - **a path's meaning is the sequence of its ties**, which can be said in words: "the task made from the message that told of this payment, in the project …". That sentence is the explanation the walk owes the person.
- A list of allowed meta-paths would be brittle as new kinds arrive (own inference). Weights per kind of tie plus the stop at collections (§2.4) give the same effect with one rule each.

### 2.6 Nearness in time
- Time is one of the cues people use most to re-find their own things: Stuff I've Seen's field deployment found "time and people" to be important retrieval cues (Dumais et al. 2003, §3.1 below; field study, abstract). In admin, what happened near the entry point's date is more likely to belong to the same episode (a bill, its reminder, the payment, the reply) than what is a year apart (own inference).
- A factor on the date difference is cheap and explainable ("from the same week"), but it must never hide a strong tie: a contract signed three years ago is the source of this month's movement. So time **orders** what a collection holds and breaks ties between equal paths; it does not cut a direct tie.

### 2.7 Which scoring, and why (own inference from 2.1–2.6)

| Method | Explainable to the person | Cheap on a phone | Stable when "+" expands one node | Hubs | Kinds of ties |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DOI, greedy subgraph (Furnas 1986; van Ham and Perer 2009) | yes: a few named terms | yes: O(S log S) on an in-memory index | yes, when D is measured from the entry point only | not by itself (their API *raises* hubs) | through the edge interest `EI` |
| Spreading activation with constraints (Crestani 1997) | yes: each constraint is a sentence | yes | yes, with a fixed focus | the fan-out constraint | the path constraint |
| Random walk with restart, personalised PageRank (Tong et al. 2006; Andersen et al. 2006) | no: a stationary probability | yes in its push form, O(1/(εα)) | no if re-run with a new seed; yes if computed once | by itself (1/degree), plus `p/d` | through weighted transitions |
| Meta-paths, PathSim (Sun et al. 2011) | yes: a path is a sentence | yes for short paths | yes | no | it is the method |

**Recommended: "the strongest chain"**, a best-first walk from the entry point that is spreading activation with Crestani's constraints, computed as van Ham and Perer's greedy subgraph, with kinds of ties weighted as `EI` and meta-paths suggest:

1. **Score of a thing** = the strength of its strongest chain of ties from the entry point: the product of its ties' strengths, times `δ = 0.75` for each step after the first. Computed best-first (Dijkstra on −log strength) over the in-memory tie index. **Its explanation is that chain**, said in words: "made from the message that told of this payment · in the project Flat".
2. **Strength of a tie, by its kind** (starting values, to be tuned on real data):
   - 1.0: tied by hand; made from / what it made (`via`, `source`); its appointment / its preparation; a direct reply (`answers`); a step of / its steps; waits for / frees; a note that describes it; a movement's source matched for sure; the task made for a letter's date or a paper's renewal;
   - 0.8: a movement's source matched as probable; a contract's preset or paper; whom a letter or a paper is for; a holder's stand-in event; instalments and orders in parts ("together");
   - 0.6: in the project (REFID or a tie); a task's contact, an event's guest, a message's sender or recipient; a note that mentions it; the budget a movement counts in; the person a site is opened for;
   - 0.4: in a project by its routes alone (not tied); a project's client by name; a contact by a phone number (texts, calls).
3. **Collections stop the walk** (Crestani's fan-out constraint, made a rule): a project, a contact, a person, a budget and a site are shown when reached, with their count, and never walked through. The entry point is the exception.
4. **Fan-out inside things**: leaving a thing with `d` ties to follow, each one is weighed `min(1, √(4/d))` (a task with 16 steps passes half; one with 100, a fifth). Between the walk's 1/d and no damping; a guess to be tuned.
5. **Bounds**: at most 4 steps from the entry point; a score under 0.1 is not shown; at most 5 things shown under each thing (van Ham and Perer's "n < 5" directions); at most **24 things on a computer and 12 on a phone** in all (§3.4 for why).
6. **Time orders, never cuts**: among a collection's members (the entry point a project, or a collection opened by "+"), and between equal scores, the nearest in time to the entry point's date comes first (today when the entry point has none).
7. **Ties broken the same way every time**: score, then the nearest in time to the entry point's date (things without a date after), then the kind's order (`Kind`), then the title, then the address. The same data gives the same picture.
8. **"+N" on each thing** = how many of its ties are not shown (left out by a bound, a collection's stop or the cap of 5), not counting the tie to the thing it hangs from, nor things shown elsewhere in the picture. Pressed, it adds the next 8 by the same score (a collection's members by strength, then time), each press 8 more; "−" folds them back. **The scores are never computed again for "+"**: it reveals more of the ranking made from the entry point, so nothing already shown moves or leaves (van Ham and Perer observed the same stability for DOI from a fixed focus). Its accessible name says what it holds: "12 more: 9 messages, 3 tasks".

9. **Inside one collection** (the entry point, or one whose "+" is pressed), one of each kind first (a letter, a task, a note, a message, a payment, then the others), then the rest by date, and at most two messages from one sender before the others. The first trial on a person's own data (5 November 2026) found a project's five newest messages hiding its letters, tasks and notes, and a weekly notification crowding out an office's letters.
10. **Shown greedily, not cut afterwards**: each thing comes under a thing already shown, the strongest first, while that thing has room for it (van Ham and Perer's greedy subgraph); a thing whose strongest chain's thing is full comes by its next chain shown.

Why not the walk with restart: it is the better ranking on paper (several paths add up), but its scores cannot be told in a sentence and they shift with every new tie. If a trial on real data shows the strongest chain missing things the person wanted, the walk (push form, ranked by `p/d`) is the fallback, its result still explained by each node's strongest chain. Which of the two ranks better on a person's own admin is **not settled by any source found**: only a trial can settle it (§8).

## 3. Graph views in personal information management

### 3.1 What the PIM research found
- **People re-find by small steps from what they know, more than by searching.**
  - Teevan, Alvarado, Ackerman and Karger (2004), "The perfect search engine is not enough: a study of orienteering behavior in directed search", CHI 2004, pp. 415–422, doi:10.1145/985692.985745 (a modified diary study of searches in e-mail, files and the web; qualitative with counts). Most directed searches were not keyword searches but "orienteering": small local steps guided by context, more among people whose information was not neatly organised.
  - Bergman, Beyth-Marom, Nachmias, Gradovitch and Whittaker (2008), "Improved search engines and navigation preference in personal information management", *ACM TOIS* 26(4), doi:10.1145/1402256.1402259 (field studies comparing desktop search engines on Windows and Mac; self-report). People estimated they navigated for 56–68 % of file retrievals and searched for 4–15 %; a better search engine changed that little; search was a last resort when the place was forgotten.
  - Whittaker, Matthews, Cerruti, Badenes and Tang (2011), "Am I wasting my time organizing email? A study of email refinding", CHI 2011, doi:10.1145/1978942.1979457 (instrumented client, 345 long-term users, over 85,000 re-finding actions; field study, logs). Preparing folders did not improve re-finding; search and **threads** (a reply chain kept together) did.
- **What people remember of a message is its gist, its reason, its sender and roughly when.** Elsweiler, Baillie and Ruthven (2008), "Exploring memory in email re-finding", *ACM TOIS* 26(4), article 21, doi:10.1145/1402256.1402260 (a study of 34 students and a group of researchers re-finding in their own mail; qualitative with counts): the topic was remembered in 85.1 % of tasks, the reason it was sent in 80.9 %, the sender in 77.1 %, something about time in 57.5 %; the sender faded most with age (95.6 % for recent mail, 56.7 % for old), and the authors conclude that "re-finding tools should rely less on the sender attribute for older information". For the graph: a thing's **reason** (the tie's kind, said in words) and its **time** are the labels worth showing; a correspondent is a weak path to old mail.
- **One index of everything seen, with time and people as cues.** Dumais, Cutrell, Cadiz, Jancke, Sarin and Robbins (2003), "Stuff I've Seen: a system for personal information retrieval and re-use", SIGIR 2003, pp. 72–79, doi:10.1145/860435.860451 (internal deployment to more than 230 people; field study, abstract read, full text not found): a unified index across mail, files, web pages and appointments; "time and people are important retrieval cues"; people found things more easily and used other tools less.
- **A graph underneath, lists on the screen.** Karger, Bakshi, Huynh, Quan and Sinha (2005), "Haystack: a customizable general-purpose information management tool for end users of semistructured data", CIDR 2005 (a system paper; read): everything is RDF, "a graph in which the nodes denote the information items", chosen because "human beings seek information by orienteering" and RDF's statements "form natural associative links along which a user can orienteer". Yet Haystack shows each object as a view whose attributes are the related objects, each one click away, with "lenses" (property lists) the person can choose; it draws no node-link diagram of the graph. The graph is the data model, browsing it is a list per object.
- **The semantic desktop and its cost.** NEPOMUK (EU project, 2006–2008) and its KDE implementation kept a desktop-wide RDF store (Virtuoso). KDE replaced it with Baloo from KDE Applications 4.13 (2014) because it "was not delivering the performance and user experience that had initially been anticipated", data having to be kept in step between Akonadi and Nepomuk by a process of its own (Wikipedia, "NEPOMUK (software)"; KDE's Nepomuk mailing list, December 2013, https://mail.kde.org/pipermail/nepomuk/2013-December/004858.html; grey literature, not a study). Its research prototype Gnowsis was evaluated in deployments with user feedback (Sauermann et al. 2006, "Semantic Desktop 2.0: the Gnowsis experience", ISWC 2006, LNCS 4273, doi:10.1007/11926078_64; abstract only). The lesson for Sioul matches its own choice against Akonadi (design.md, "Kontact"): no second store of copies; the tie index is built in memory from the files that already hold the ties.

### 3.2 How people use graph views in note tools
- **Obsidian** (vendor documentation, https://obsidian.md/help/plugins/graph, read 5 November 2026): a global graph of every note and a **local graph** of the notes tied to the active one, with a depth slider ("each depth level adds notes linked to the notes shown at the previous level"), filters (search, tags, attachments, existing files only, orphans), colour groups by search, arrows, a text fade threshold, and forces (centre, repel, link, distance). Depth is the only relevance control: no notion of a tie's kind, of hubs, or of a budget.
- **What its users report**: in an interview and observation study of seven researchers using Obsidian with their own vaults, the authors describe four ways of re-finding: the search field, the tag list, tags in a note's text and links in a note's text; the graph view is not one of them (Ferreira, Segura, Souza and Brasil 2025, "How people manage knowledge in their 'second brains': a case study with industry researchers using Obsidian", arXiv:2509.20187, doi:10.1007/978-3-032-05008-3_15; qualitative, read). In Obsidian's forum thread "What's the point of the graph view?" (2023, https://forum.obsidian.md/t/whats-the-point-of-the-graph-view-how-are-you-using-it/71316; grey literature), about five of eighteen replies use it, for an overview, for untagged notes or the local graph while writing ("really helpful when I need it to be (which isn't often)"), about four rarely or never ("never seen the point of graph view other than 'hey look at this!'"), and several name the layout that moves on every load and cannot be pinned. **This is the known failure: a picture to admire, not a tool to act from.** No controlled study of graph views in note tools was found.
- **TheBrain** (vendor help, Android, https://help.thebrain.com/androidphone/index.html, and older manuals; vendor documentation): the "plex" puts the active thought in the middle, its parents above, its children below, its siblings to the right and its "jumps" (other ties) to the left; clicking a thought makes it the centre and the plex rearranges around it. Where a thing appears says how it is tied, and the layout is the same for the same ties: **stable by role, not by physics**.
- **Tinderbox** (Eastgate; Bernstein's own descriptions, https://eastgate.com/Tinderbox/who.html; vendor documentation): a spatial hypertext in which the writer places notes on a map by hand, "clustering as well as linking"; an automatic "Gaudí" view lets related notes attract each other. The map is the person's own arrangement, the opposite of a layout computed afresh.

### 3.3 The readability of node-link diagrams
- **Ghoniem, Fekete and Castagliola (2004)**, "A comparison of the readability of graphs using node-link and matrix-based representations", InfoVis 2004, pp. 17–24, doi:10.1109/INFOVIS.2004.1; journal version in *Information Visualization* 4(2), 2005 (controlled experiment, seven generic tasks). For graphs above about twenty nodes, matrices were read better on most tasks; **finding a path was the task that favoured node-link diagrams**.
- **Yoghourdjian, Yang, Dwyer, Lawrence, Wybrow and Marriott (2021)**, "Scalability of network visualisation from a cognitive load perspective", *IEEE TVCG* 27(2), doi:10.1109/TVCG.2020.3030459 (controlled experiment on shortest paths, 25 to 175 nodes, with EEG, pupil size and heart rate). People "have significant difficulty finding the shortest path in high-density node-link diagrams with more than 50 nodes and even low-density graphs with more than 100 nodes"; load rose with difficulty, then fell, as if people gave up; the layout's global features (size, crossings) mattered more than the path's own.
- **Purchase (1997)**, "Which aesthetic has the greatest effect on human understanding?", Graph Drawing 1997, LNCS 1353, pp. 248–261, doi:10.1007/3-540-63938-1_67 (controlled experiment): reducing edge crossings mattered most; bends and symmetry less.
- **Moving drawings**: keeping the drawing stable (the "mental map") helped people follow particular nodes or long paths in graphs that change; animation helped when the drawing was unstable and the important nodes could not be highlighted (Archambault and Purchase 2016, "Can animation support the visualisation of dynamic graphs?", *Information Sciences* 330, pp. 495–509, doi:10.1016/j.ins.2015.04.017; controlled experiment, abstract read). Elsewhere preserving it had little effect on errors and times (Archambault, Purchase and Pinaud 2011, *IEEE TVCG* 17(4), doi:10.1109/TVCG.2010.78). The evidence is mixed; nothing in it argues *for* a layout that moves.

### 3.4 When a graph helps, and when a list serves better (own inference from 3.1–3.3)
- **Following ties is a path task**: the chain of ties from one thing (a movement → its message → the task made from it → the task's project). Path finding is the one task where node-link diagrams beat other forms (Ghoniem et al.), and only while they stay small (Yoghourdjian et al.). A drawn neighbourhood earns its place there.
- **Looking something up and opening it is a list task**: "what is tied to this message? open the task" is what the Related panel already does, one click per row, as Haystack showed each object's related items. The PIM studies favour this kind of stepping (orienteering, navigation, threads).
- **So both, from one walk**: the graph and an outline (an indented tree list) show the same things, the same chains, the same "+N", in the same order. The outline is the text alternative for screen readers and keyboards, the default on a phone, and the place to read long titles; the graph is the default on a computer, for seeing a chain at a glance. A switch moves between them, keeping the focus on the same thing.
- **Sizes**: 24 things at most on a computer, under the 50 where shortest paths became hard even in dense drawings and near Ghoniem's twenty; an ego tree is sparse, which leaves margin. 12 on a phone, whose screen holds about a quarter of a laptop window's area (own inference: no study measured graph readability on phones that was found). "+" lets the person go past these on purpose.
- **Against eye candy**: no global graph of everything (it answers no question of admin); a layout placed by the tie's role, never re-run by physics on each opening; every node opens its card; nothing shown that cannot be acted on.

## 4. Telling each kind apart: shape, icon, colour, words

### 4.1 Sources
- **Colour alone is never the cue.** WCAG 2.2 (W3C Recommendation, 2023; standard): 1.4.1 *Use of Color* (A): colour is not the only visual means of conveying information; 1.4.11 *Non-text Contrast* (AA): parts of graphics needed to understand them reach 3:1 against what is next to them; 1.1.1 *Non-text Content* (A); 1.3.1 *Info and Relationships* (A); 2.1.1 *Keyboard* (A); 2.4.3 *Focus Order* (A); 2.4.7 *Focus Visible* (AA); 2.5.8 *Target Size (Minimum)* (AA): 24 × 24 CSS pixels; 4.1.2 *Name, Role, Value* (A). https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/
- **How many colours can be told apart.** Healey (1996), "Choosing effective colours for data visualization", IEEE Visualization '96, pp. 263–270 (controlled experiment with 38 observers): a method to choose colours for rapid identification; it cites a guideline of five to seven colours at once from military display research (anecdotal, as the paper itself says). Tol (2021), "Colour schemes", SRON/EPS/TN/09-002, issue 3.2 (technical note; read through the khroma package's summary, https://cran.r-project.org/package=khroma): the "bright", "high-contrast", "vibrant" and "muted" qualitative schemes are colour-blind safe; "muted" has nine colours. Sioul has **seventeen kinds** (`links::Kind`, plus the movement it lacks): far more than colours can carry.
- **Graphs for screen readers.** ARIA's linear reading of a visualisation with many elements is slow, so navigable structures (lists, trees, graphs) are built beside the drawing (Elavsky, Nadolskis and Moritz 2023, "Data Navigator: an accessibility-centered data navigation toolkit", IEEE VIS 2023, *IEEE TVCG* 30(1), arXiv:2308.08475; a toolkit with case examples, abstract read). The W3C's tree view pattern gives the keys a tree's reader expects (WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices, "Tree View", https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/treeview/; standard practice): ↓ and ↑ move to the next and previous node without opening any; → opens a closed node, or moves into an open one; ← closes an open node, or moves to the parent; Enter does the node's default action; Home and End; type-ahead.
- **Qt.** `Accessible.role` takes `QAccessible::Role`, which has `Tree`, `TreeItem`, `List`, `ListItem`, `Graphic` and `Link` (Qt 6 documentation, `QAccessible`; vendor documentation). `Accessible.expanded` and `Accessible.expandable` exist from Qt 6.12, `Accessible.announce()` from Qt 6.8 (Qt 6 documentation, "Accessible QML Type"). Sioul builds with Qt 6.9 or newer and runs 6.11 (docs/qt-quick.md): the folded or unfolded state must be said in the name or description until 6.12.

### 4.2 What follows (own inference)
- **Three cues, from coarse to fine, each enough alone**:
  1. **a shape per family** (six families, seen before reading anything): *time and doing* (task, event): a rounded square; *messages* (mail, draft, letter): a wide rounded rectangle; *people* (contact, person): a circle; *money* (movement, budget line, invoice, contract): a diamond; *records* (note, paper, file, site, web page): a square with a folded corner; *projects*: a hexagon, the collection that gathers the others;
  2. **an icon per kind**, the theme's `kindIcons` completed (person, paper, contract, letter, invoice, site and movement have none today and fall back to the link icon);
  3. **a colour per family**, six tints of the theme's calm palette checked colour-blind safe (Tol's "muted" scheme as the reference, adapted to Theme.qml's light and dark values), each node's outline at 3:1 against the background.
- **The kind in words** wherever the thing is named: the outline's second line, and every node's accessible name ("Task: Pay the rent; made from the message 'Rent for November'; 3 more"). The kinds' words exist already (`taskview::related_views` gives `how` in the person's language); "Task", "Message"… need words of their own in the Fluent files.
- **The entry point** drawn larger, with a double outline; **focus** with the theme's `focus` colour, 2 pixels, never only a colour change; **a thing not found** (a tie to something deleted) dashed and dimmed, as the Related panel dims it.
- **One set of keys for both views**, the tree view pattern's: ↓ ↑ in the outline's order (the graph's focus moves the same way), → opens "+" or goes to the first thing under, ← folds or goes back up, Enter opens the card, Home goes to the entry point, the menu key (or a long press) opens the thing's menu (open, open its graph, undo this tie), type-ahead by title. Tab leaves the graph.

## 5. Layout

### 5.1 Sources
- **Radial layouts**: the focus in the middle, each ring one step further, each subtree in its own wedge (Eades 1992, "Drawing free trees", *Bulletin of the Institute for Combinatorics and its Applications* 5; theory, cited through Yee et al.). Yee, Fisher, Dhamija and Hearst (2001), "Animated exploration of dynamic graphs with radial layout", InfoVis 2001, pp. 43–50, https://bailando.berkeley.edu/papers/infovis01.htm (a system; abstract and method read; its DOI was not confirmed): interpolating positions in polar coordinates, with constraints keeping each new layout near the previous one, makes a change of focus easy to follow; adding or removing a node disturbs its siblings only slightly.
- **Radial against layered trees**: Burch, Konevtsova, Heinrich, Höferlin and Weiskopf (2011), "Evaluation of traditional, orthogonal, and radial tree diagrams by an eye tracking study", *IEEE TVCG* 17(12), pp. 2440–2448, doi:10.1109/TVCG.2011.193 (controlled experiment with eye tracking, 38 participants): traditional (layered) and orthogonal tree layouts were significantly better than radial ones for finding the common ancestor of marked leaves; with radial layouts people cross-checked their answer more often.
- **Force-directed layouts** move on each run unless constrained: van Ham and Perer chose stress majorization for "far better layout stability", and Obsidian's users name a layout that moves on every load and cannot be pinned (§3.2).
- **Crossings** matter most to understanding (Purchase 1997, §3.3).
- **TheBrain** places by role (parents above, children below, siblings right, other ties left): the same ties always land in the same zone (§3.2).

### 5.2 What follows (own inference)
- **A layered tree, read from the left, rather than a radial or a force-directed drawing.** The walk of §2.7 gives each thing one strongest chain, so the things shown form a tree hanging from the entry point; it is drawn as a tidy tree: the entry point at the left, one column per step (1 to 4), each thing beside the thing it hangs from, its siblings in the walk's order (score, then kind, then date). Why:
  - the chain reads like a sentence, left to right: "this payment → its message → the task made from it → the project";
  - titles are horizontal and get the width they need (a radial layout turns or crowds them);
  - layered trees did better than radial ones in the one controlled comparison found (Burch et al. 2011);
  - it is the outline (§3.4) turned sideways: the phone's outline and the computer's drawing are one structure, one order, one set of keys;
  - nothing in it is computed by physics: the same ties give the same picture every time.
- **Ties that are not the tree's** (a second chain between two things already shown: the task is also in the project the movement's budget belongs to) are drawn as thin curves, under the tree's own ties, and never move anything. They are what makes it a network and not only a list.
- **The tie's kind on each tree edge**, in the Related panel's words ("made from", "its appointment", "in the project"), small and muted, along the horizontal stretch of the edge; a cross-tie's words only on hover or focus. Where a column is too narrow for its words, they go to the thing's second line instead.
- **"+" never moves what is above or beside**: the things it adds go under the thing pressed, in its next column; what is below slides down (200 ms), the thing pressed keeps its place on the screen (the view scrolls with it), and "−" gives back the same picture as before. This is the stability an outline has, and the mental-map research asks no more (§3.3).
- **Sizes on a computer**: a thing's box is about 220 × 44 pixels (its icon in its shape, its title elided at about 28 characters, its kind and date beneath), columns 300 pixels apart with the tie's words in the gap; 4 columns fit 1,280 pixels. Pan with a drag or the wheel, zoom with Ctrl + wheel or a pinch, "Fit" to see it whole.
- **On a phone**: the outline is the view (each row a thing, indented by its step, its tie's words on its second line, "+N" at its right end, 48 dp tall at least). "Chain" draws the same tree, two columns at a time across the screen's width, scrolled sideways one column per swipe; a pinch zooms. Rows are what a thumb handles well; a drawing of 12 boxes on 412 pixels is not (no study of graph views on phones was found).

## 6. The build in Qt Quick and Rust

### 6.1 Sources
- **Qt Quick Shapes** (Qt 6 documentation, "Shape QML Type", read 5 November 2026; vendor documentation): the geometry renderer triangulates on the CPU, the curve renderer (asked through `preferredRendererType`, from Qt 6.6) draws curves on the GPU with its own antialiasing; changing a path re-triangulates it, so animating paths "can affect performance on less powerful systems", and a change to one `ShapePath` reprocesses that path only; "prefer using one Shape item with multiple ShapePath elements over multiple Shape items"; `asynchronous: true` moves the preparation off the main thread.
- **Qt Quick TreeView** (Qt 6.3+) "only accepts a model that inherits QAbstractItemModel" (Qt 6 documentation, "TreeView QML Type"); Sioul hands QML JSON arrays, as the Related panel does (`sioul.related(uri)`).
- **Sioul's own measurements** (docs/android.md, "Pages and scrolling"; docs/qt-quick.md): on the 2019 test phone, making QML objects is what costs; with Qt 6.11 every item shown, hidden or restacked makes Qt walk the window's whole tree at the next frame; pages make their parts at first use (`Later.qml`).
- **The app's cache** (`crates/sioul-app/src/work.rs`, `loaded()`): `Loaded` is kept in an `Arc` until the next change; cards never read the stores on the window's thread (`loaded_known`).
- **Libraries**: QuickQanava (a Qt Quick graph library, BSD-3-Clause, https://github.com/cneben/QuickQanava) and QtNodes (Qt Widgets' graphics view, https://github.com/paceholder/nodeeditor) exist; both are made for editing diagrams by hand (nodes dragged, ports, connectors), not for a computed read-only neighbourhood, and the second is not Qt Quick (vendor documentation, own reading of their READMEs' scope; not tested).

### 6.2 What follows (own inference)
- **Rust computes everything, QML draws**: the tie index, the walk, the tree, each thing's column and row, the cross-ties, each "+N" and its words, as one JSON answer per ask (`sioul.askChain(uri, opened, phone)`, answered by `chainReady`, on a worker thread; `opened` the things whose "+" was pressed). The layout of a tidy tree is a few dozen lines (columns by step, rows by a post-order count); no library is needed.
- **The tie index is built off the window's thread**, with `Loaded`, the first time a graph or a Related panel asks after a change, and kept beside it in the same `Arc`; the Related panel then reads from it too, which ends its full pass per call (§1.1). Movements are derived once per build, over the window the budgets page uses.
- **Its memory**: ties held as pairs of small integers into one table of addresses, not as `String` pairs (a mailbox of 20,000 messages with two references and two addresses each gives about 100,000 ties: about 1.2 MB of pairs plus the addresses, against tens of MB as strings; own estimate, to be measured on the phone, which holds about 140 MB in the background).
- **Drawing**: each thing an `Item` (its shape, an `Icon`, two `Label`s, the "+N" button), at most 24 plus what "+" added, made by a `Repeater` over the JSON, its position bound to the column and row with a 200 ms `Behavior`; all edges in **one** `Shape` with one `ShapePath` per edge (`PathCubic` for a cross-tie, `PathLine`s for the tree), `preferredRendererType: Shape.CurveRenderer`; positions change only on "+" or "−", so nothing re-triangulates while the page rests. A `Flickable` pans; a `PinchHandler` and Ctrl + wheel zoom by scale. About 100 items in all: within what a phone page already makes.
- **No `TreeView`** for the outline: a `ListView` of the same JSON rows, each with its depth, `reuseItems: true`, as Sioul's other lists, with `Accessible.role: Accessible.TreeItem`; the folded or unfolded state said in `Accessible.description` until Qt 6.12's `Accessible.expanded`.
- **Opening a card**: every node and row calls `window.openThing(item, true)` (main.qml), which already opens every kind where it lives (the card host for a task, an event, a dated thing; Mail's reader for a message; the right page for the rest) and keeps the graph as the card it came from (`CardHost.follow`). The movement needs its branch there (it has none: `kind_of` gives `Other` today).
- **A page, not a side panel**: four columns need the window's width, and the card host already opens beside the page on a computer; so the graph is a page of its own ("Ties of …"), Back returning to where it was opened, and each card opened from it beside it. On a phone, the outline is the page, a card over it, Back to the outline.
- **Opened from everywhere by one button**: `ThingActions.qml` (the buttons every card ends with) gets a third, "Ties" (an icon of the theme's, the words for screen readers), and the Related panel's heading a "See them all" link; the pages whose cards have no `ThingActions` (a movement, a paper, a contract, a letter, an invoice, a person, a site, a draft, a budget line) get "Ties" in their menu. Each thing's right-click or long-press menu gets "Its ties". A keyboard shortcut on any open card opens it: Ctrl + G (free today; Ctrl + L already forwards a message in the Reader).

## 7. Gates: what the graph never shows or follows

From the code's existing rules (`crates/sioul-cli/src/mcp/access.rs`, `crates/sioul-core/src/consent.rs`, docs/mcp.md "Per-project access", `porch::Gate`, `RecentsGuard.qml`) and own inference where marked.

### 7.1 In the person's own window
- **Everything that is the person's is shown**, as on its own page: the graph adds no secrecy the pages do not have.
- **Health**: shown, and the window's `RecentsGuard` turns on while a health thing is on the graph page (an errand's task, its appointment, a prescription's date, a dose), as it does for the health cards today (`Consent::keep_errands` already computes that set: reuse it). Own inference: a graph opened from a gas bill should not put a pharmacy errand in Android's overview of apps.
- **Another's agenda shared for availability alone** (`agendas::Agenda::busy`, `Consent::keep_private`): its events show as the agenda shows them ("Busy") and are never walked through: their ties are another person's.
- **Mail the Porch sets aside** (spam, forged, hostile, an unproven sender claiming a client): computed ties go only through mail the gate admits, as `projectview::mail_of` and `Threads::through` already do ("a forged message citing two conversations does not join them"); such a message reached by a tie made by hand shows with the Porch's own words and is not walked through.
- **The web, files, programs**: shown as ends, never walked through, never fetched to find more ties; a program opens its folder (`World::describe`, `openable`, `is_program`), as everywhere.
- **The tie index stays in memory**: never written to a file, a cache, the sharing log or a log line. It gathers in one place the ties of sealed stores (persons, papers, health) with the plain ones; written out, it would undo their sealing. Own inference.

### 7.2 For agents, if their `links` tool reuses the walk
- **The walk runs on what the agent may see, at every step**: a thing kept from agents (a closed project's, a thing in no project while those are closed, a kept work account's mail, a closed person's, an availability-only event, health) is **neither shown nor walked through**. Walking through it would show the agent that an open thing is tied to a closed one, and the chain's words would name it ("made from the message …"). This is stricter than filtering the result afterwards, which is what `open_related` does at one step today, and it is what one step already amounts to.
- **What was left out is counted, never named**: one number for the things kept from agents that are tied to the things shown (`access::left_out`), the same sentence as today. "+N" counts only what the agent may see.
- **Mail as agents see it**: subjects masked and hostile senders unnamed (`mail_shown`), in the chain's words too.
- **Bounds for agents**: depth 2 by default, 3 at most, 30 things at most; the same scores, so that the person and the agent see the same chain of what they both may see.
- **No new write**: the walk reads; `link` stays the one tool that writes a tie, with its rules (`Loaded::tie`, never into a meeting with others).

## 8. What only a trial with real data can settle
- **Whether the strongest chain shows what the person wants** in its 24 things. No source measured relevance in a person's admin; the strengths of §2.7 are starting values. The trial: the person opens the graph from a few kinds of things (a movement, a message, a letter, a project, a contact), says what is missing or useless; the strengths are tuned in code. No rating chore: what the person says while using it is the measure (docs: "no validation chores"). If the strongest chain keeps missing things reached by several weaker paths, try the walk with restart (push form, ranked by `p/d`) on the same entry points.
- **The fan-out damping** (`√(4/d)`) and whether stopping at collections is too strict (a contact with three messages may be worth walking through).
- **The sizes**: 24 on a computer, 12 on a phone, 5 under a thing, 8 per "+": guesses bounded by the readability studies, not measured on admin data.
- **The cost on the phone**: building the index (with movements derived) and its memory on the 2019 test phone with a real mailbox; the page's first opening and each "+" under 100 ms on the window's thread (the layout is computed off it).
- **Screen readers**: the outline read by TalkBack on Android and Orca on Linux, the folded state said, the keys of the tree pattern working in both views.
- **Whether the drawing earns its place**: on the computer, whether the person uses the drawing or the outline; if the outline wins, the drawing stays a switch away, not the default.
