# Persons: the people a household's admin is done for

**The question**: the person keeps papers, agendas, letters and money not only for themself but for people who depend on them or share their household: a child, an elderly parent, a partner. Today Sioul names these people in four unconnected places (a paper's `holder` text, an agenda's `name` and `parent`, a holder line's CN and address, a contact card). Should a person become one object across the app, distinct from a contact; what may Sioul keep about them and do for them; which French household notions do papers follow; which standards can carry it; and what do family apps take for a person?

Researched on 4 November 2026. Built by 5 November 2026 ([persons.md](../persons.md)): persons as Sioul's own records linked to cards; the papers, agendas and letters they are for; legal measures; the end of a relation and the hand-over; budgets and sites for a person; the household on the Contacts page; and what agents see of them. It builds on [agendas.md](agendas.md) (parents sharing care across two homes, partners), which is not redone here. The family in the examples is fictional: Alex, Sam, their children Léa and Tom, and Alex's mother Odile.

**How evidence is graded**: **trial**; **meta** (systematic review or meta-analysis); **survey** (cross-sectional or longitudinal, correlational); **qualitative** (interviews, focus groups); **theory** (a conceptual or normative work); **legal text** (a statute, a regulation, a treaty, a court ruling, an official guidance); **official** (an administration's own help pages); **standard** (an RFC, a W3C or HL7 specification); **vendor** (an app's own documentation, not tested here); **code** (a server's source, read). "Reading" lines are design inferences, not findings; "(unverified)" marks what was not confirmed at a primary source.

## 0. What Sioul holds today (read in the code, main 7cb6b3d)
- **Papers** (`crates/sioul-core/src/papers.rs`, `docs/papers.md`): `Paper::holder`, a free text, "whose, in a household", `#[serde(default)]`. The wallet is `sioul-papers.toml` at the notes folder's root, travelling in the "papers" part (`PAPERS_RULES`: each `[[paper]]` keyed by `id`, each field an entry). `Wallet::write` writes the known fields only, and `Paper` keeps no unknown field: **an older Sioul that rewrites the wallet drops any field it does not know**. The MCP's `papers` tool gives "who it is for" (`docs/mcp.md`).
- **Agendas** (`agendas.rs`): `Whose` (yours, child, parent, partner, shared); `Said::name` ("whose name: a child's above all"), `Said::parent` and `parent_address` (the other parent of a child's agenda), `parent_answers`, `holders` per kind; kept in `[agenda.calendars."<place>"]` in the configuration, travelling with "settings", one entry per field. `Agenda::person` is the name, `Agenda::group()` groups the filter by (whose, lowercased name): two agendas of one child group together only by spelling.
- **Holders** (`holders.rs`): `X-SIOUL-TAKEN-BY;CN=<name>;PARTSTAT=…;SINCE=…:mailto:<address>` on a child's event or task; `holders::Who` is `Me` or `Other` (the one other parent the agenda names); `custody()` matches all-day "With <name>" titles against your names and the other parent's by lowercased text.
- **Contacts** (`contacts.rs`): vCards read from the address books; new cards written in vCard 3.0; `as_vcard3` drops `KIND`, `MEMBER`, `RELATED`, `GENDER`, `ANNIVERSARY`… for servers that take only 3.0 (Google). `CATEGORIES` are shown as Nextcloud's groups. Nothing reads `RELATED` or `KIND` today.
- **Do-not-disturb's people** (`everywhere.rs`, `dnd-people.toml`): `Person { id, name, phones, emails, contact }` with `#[serde(flatten)] other` keeping what a later version writes: the one existing "person" record, by a stable id, tied to a card by its UID. It is the pattern to follow.
- **Ties** (`links.rs`): every thing has an address (`sioul:paper/<id>`, `sioul:contact/<UID>`, `sioul:letter/<id>`…); a tie lives in the thing that makes it where its format allows, else in `data/links.toml` (part "settings", `LINKS_RULES`). There is no `sioul:person/` address yet.
- **Letters** (`letters.rs`): a `Reading` has sender, kind, amount, dates, appointment, `reference` (the person's number with the sender); no addressee. **Budgets**, **health** and **capacity** name nobody but the person.
- **Reading**: four places hold names as text, matched by spelling, and two (`holders`, `agendas`) allow exactly one other adult. A person object replaces the spellings by one id; the texts stay as what older versions read.

## 1. For whom the admin is done, and how each relation differs

### 1.1 A child: authority that shrinks as the child grows, and ends at eighteen
- **French law: parental authority exists for the child, includes the child's private life, and associates the child "according to age and maturity".** Art. 371-1 of the Civil Code: parental authority is a set of rights and duties "having as its end the interest of the child", to protect them "in their safety, health, private life and morality" (the words "sa vie privée" added by law no. 2024-120 of 19 February 2024); parents "associate the child with the decisions that concern them, according to their age and degree of maturity". Art. 372-1 (same law): parents protect the child's image rights together, and associate the child with them. Art. 414: majority at eighteen, "at that age everyone is capable of exercising the rights they enjoy".
  - Code civil, art. 371-1, 372-1, 414. https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/texte_lc/LEGITEXT000006070721/ — legal text. Loi n° 2024-120 du 19 février 2024 visant à garantir le respect du droit à l'image des enfants (JORF 20 February 2024), art. 1. https://www.dalloz-actualite.fr/document/loi-n-2024-120-19-fevr-2024-jo-20-fevr — legal text (read through Dalloz Actualité and Le Club des juristes; Légifrance not fetched).
  - **Reading**: a child is a person whose papers and agendas the person keeps *for* them, with a duty to the child's own privacy, and a hand-over date written in law. Sioul should know each child's birth date only to say, at sixteen and eighteen, what passes to the child (§4: ameli, Mon espace santé), never to count anything else.
- **Children want a say, and shared residence makes it harder** (Haugen 2010, in [agendas.md](agendas.md) §2.2). A child's agenda belongs to the child; an older child may read or write it.

### 1.2 An elderly parent: support, not substitution, and the law's forms
- **The treaty: support for a person's own decisions, never in their place.** CRPD art. 12: persons with disabilities "enjoy legal capacity on an equal basis with others in all aspects of life" (§2); States provide "access … to the support they may require in exercising their legal capacity" (§3); safeguards ensure measures "respect the rights, will and preferences of the person, are free of conflict of interest and undue influence, are proportional and tailored … apply for the shortest time possible and are subject to regular review" (§4); and equal right "to control their own financial affairs" (§5). The Committee's General Comment No. 1 reads support as never amounting to substitute decision-making, and replaces "best interests" with "the best interpretation of will and preferences".
  - United Nations (2006). Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, art. 12. https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-12-equal-recognition-before-the-law.html — legal text (treaty; France ratified in 2010).
  - Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2014). General comment No. 1, Article 12: Equal recognition before the law, CRPD/C/GC/1, §§17, 21. https://undocs.org/CRPD/C/GC/1 — legal text (the treaty body's interpretation, not binding; §17 as quoted in the literature, not fetched).
- **France was told in 2021 that its regimes substitute decisions.** The Committee was concerned at "deprivation of legal capacity and autonomy through guardianship and wardship, on the basis of medical assessments" and "the absence of supported decision-making mechanisms", and recommended "repealing provisions allowing for substituted decision-making" and moving resources "to develop supported decision-making mechanisms that respect the dignity, autonomy, will and preferences of persons with disabilities".
  - Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2021). Concluding observations on the initial report of France, CRPD/C/FRA/CO/1, §§25–26 (4 October 2021). https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2062451.html — legal text (read in the PDF).
- **The French forms, from lightest to heaviest** (service-public.gouv.fr, pages checked July 2026):
  - **A procuration** (a civil-law mandate, Code civil art. 1984; a bank's own procuration on an account): the person chooses, can revoke at any time, and keeps all their rights. No judge.
  - **The mandat de protection future** (Code civil art. 477 ff.): a contract the person signs in advance naming who will represent them "if they can no longer manage their interests"; it covers the person, their property or both; it takes effect only when a doctor on the prosecutor's list certifies it and the court registry stamps it; the mandataire keeps an inventory, and accounts are rendered (yearly to the notary for a notarial one); before it takes effect the person may revoke or change it at any time. Parents may make one for a dependent child with an illness or a disability.
  - **The habilitation familiale** (art. 494-1 ff.): a judge allows a close relative (ascendants, descendants, siblings, a spouse, PACS partner or cohabitant "unless shared life has ceased") to *represent* or to *assist* the person, for one act or generally (at most ten years, renewable); the person keeps their personal rights (residence, vote, marriage or PACS after informing); **no yearly account is owed**, only control "in case of dispute".
  - **The curatelle** (art. 440 ff.): the person acts alone for everyday management and with the curator's co-signature for acts of disposal; in a *curatelle renforcée* the curator receives the income, pays the expenses and hands over the rest "for their free use". The person decides alone their residence, their vote, their will, and consents to their own care when able.
  - **The tutelle** (art. 440, 473 ff.): the tutor represents the person in civil acts; the person keeps "strictly personal" acts, their residence, their relationships, their vote (never by proxy to the tutor), and is owed "clear information, adapted to their state"; at most five years (ten when no improvement is expected), lifted automatically if not reviewed in time.
  - Service-public.gouv.fr (2026). Protection juridique d'un majeur (N155); Habilitation familiale (F33367); Mandat de protection future (F16670); Curatelle (F2094); Tutelle d'un majeur (F2120). https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/N155 — official. Code civil, art. 415 (protection "favours, as far as possible, [the person's] autonomy"), 428 (necessity, subsidiarity, proportionality), 457-1, 459. https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/texte_lc/LEGITEXT000006070721/ — legal text.
- **Few studies test how to support decisions in dementia.** A PRISMA review found 11 eligible studies out of 2,348 records, heterogeneous; strategies start either from the person's remaining abilities or from the environment (who is there, where, how the question is put).
  - Wied TS, Knebel M, Tesky VA, Haberstroh J (2019). The human right to make one's own choices: implications for supported decision-making in persons with dementia. A systematic review. *European Psychologist* 24(2). https://doi.org/10.1027/1016-9040/a000372 — meta (narrative; year and issue as indexed, unverified).
- **Reading**: an elderly parent is, by default, an adult who decides; the person *helps*. Most of what a helper does needs no legal form at all (sorting the post, booking, filling a form the parent signs), and Sioul must not make a legal form look like a precondition. Where a form exists, it is a *paper* (the judgment, the mandate, the procuration: kind, date, end, review date) and a *role* the person holds towards that parent, with its end date: the tutelle's five years and the habilitation's ten are exactly Sioul's "renewing starts" rule. Sioul never records a capacity judgment as a trait of the parent ("under tutelle" is a measure with dates, not who they are), and keeps no "can decide" switch: CRPD art. 12 and the 2021 observations argue against software that encodes substitution.

### 1.3 A partner: an equal, not a dependent
- **French law gives spouses and PACS partners solidarity for the household's everyday debts, and cohabitants nothing.** Spouses: each may contract alone for "the upkeep of the household or the education of the children", and both are bound (Code civil art. 220); PACS partners likewise "for the needs of everyday life" (art. 515-4); a cohabitant (concubin, art. 515-8) has no such tie. None of these gives one partner a right to act in the other's name: a mandate is needed (art. 218 for spouses).
  - Code civil, art. 218, 220, 515-4, 515-8. https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/texte_lc/LEGITEXT000006070721/ — legal text.
- **Taxes and benefits count a couple differently**: married and PACS partners file one return (one foyer fiscal), cohabitants two; the CAF counts any couple living together, married or not (§4).
- **Partners share calendars as relationship work, and both "busy" and full sight can hurt** (Thayer et al. 2012, in [agendas.md](agendas.md) §2.3); intimate partners are the commonest abusers of monitoring tools (§3.4).
- **Reading**: a partner is not a "person who depends on" the person; most of their papers are their own, kept in their own Sioul or nowhere. Sioul should keep a partner as a person *only for what the household shares*: the joint tax notice, the lease, the shared agenda, the papers they asked the person to keep. A partner's own papers enter only with their word, said once at entry ("Sam asked you to keep it"), which is the act Sioul records. At a separation, the person takes the partner out: their papers go back with them (exported), shared documents stay as the household's past, and nothing of the partner is kept that the person could not show them.

### 1.4 The other parent of a child, in another home
Covered in [agendas.md](agendas.md) §2.2 and built (holders, `Who::Other`): most separated parents run in parallel; contentious ones use technology to control; a shared calendar invites watching. **Reading for persons**: the other parent is a person *in relation to a child*, never a member of the person's household. Sioul keeps of them what the child's agenda needs (name, address, how they answer) and nothing else; their availability stays private as it is now (finding 52).

### 1.5 What each relation means for what Sioul keeps, and for whom it speaks
| Relation | Sioul keeps for them | Speaks for them | Ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| A child | papers, agenda, health appointments, school letters, a budget's share | as their parent, in the person's own name ("for Léa") | at 18: papers and accounts handed over (§4); earlier, step by step (ameli at 16) |
| An adult the person helps (an elderly parent) | what the parent hands over or asks for | never, unless a paper (a procuration, a mandate, a judgment) says so, and only within it | when the parent says, or the measure ends (dates) |
| A partner | what the household shares, and what they ask the person to keep | never | at separation: theirs goes back with them |
| Another parent of a child | name, address, how they answer | never | with the child's agenda |
| The person themself | everything | — | — |

## 2. The administrative burden on the people who care

### 2.1 Where the load is
- **Administrative burden is learning, compliance and psychological costs, and states choose how much of it falls on people.** Herd and Moynihan's book generalises finding 3 (Moynihan, Herd & Harvey 2015): burdens are "policymaking by other means", they fall hardest on those with the fewest resources, and they are often carried by someone other than the person entitled.
  - Herd P, Moynihan DP (2018). *Administrative Burden: Policymaking by Other Means*. New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://www.russellsage.org/publications/administrative-burden — theory with case studies.
- **Health admin alone delays care for one adult in four.** In a US survey (Health Reform Monitoring Survey, March 2019), about three quarters of adults had done at least one administrative task for their care in the past year (scheduling, prior authorisations, billing, insurance disputes, getting information); 24.4 % of all respondents delayed or gave up care because of one, a rate comparable to cost barriers; adults with a disability were the most burdened.
  - Kyle MA, Frakt AB (2021). Patient administrative burden in the US health care system. *Health Services Research* 56(5):755–765. https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6773.13861 — survey (representative panel; self-report).
- **Helping with health admin is what weighs on carers.** Among 1,739 unpaid carers of 1,171 older adults with disabilities (US NHATS and NSOC 2011), those giving substantial help coordinating care and managing medications were more than five times as likely to give up valued activities as carers giving no such help, and had higher odds of emotional, physical and financial difficulty, after adjustment for the older adult's function; 45.5 % of them helped someone with dementia, and they more often lived with that person.
  - Wolff JL, Spillman BC, Freedman VA, Kasper JD (2016). A national profile of family and unpaid caregivers who assist older adults with health care activities. *JAMA Internal Medicine* 176(3):372–379. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.7664 — survey (national, cross-sectional; figures from the abstract).
- **In France: 9.3 million people help a relative regularly, and the help is getting heavier.** In 2021, 8.8 million adults and 0.5 million minors aged 5 or more said they regularly helped someone with a disability or a loss of autonomy (moral support 6.4 million, everyday life 5.7 million, money 1.3 million); 56 % are women. Between 2008 and 2022 the number of carers helping with everyday life fell by 6 % while those needing help rose, so each carer does more: the share giving 35 hours a week or more rose from 8 % to 13 %. "Managing their budget, their money or their everyday administrative steps" is one of the tasks counted, done mostly by women in both years.
  - DREES (2023). 9,3 millions de personnes déclarent apporter une aide régulière à un proche en situation de handicap ou de perte d'autonomie. *Études et résultats* 1255 (enquête Vie quotidienne et santé 2021, 334,000 respondents). https://drees.solidarites-sante.gouv.fr/publications/etudes-et-resultats/93-millions-de-personnes-declarent-apporter-une-aide-reguliere-un — official statistics (survey).
  - Verhagen A (2026). Le nombre de proches aidants à la vie quotidienne baisse de 6 % entre 2008 et 2022, et l'aide apportée s'intensifie. *Études et résultats* 1377, DREES (Handicap-Santé 2008, Autonomie-Ménages 2022). https://drees.solidarites-sante.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/2026-06/Embargo_ER1377_Proches_aidants.pdf — official statistics (survey); the women's share per task read from its chart 4.
- **The sandwich: a quarter of US adults, half of those in their forties.** In September 2025, 25 % of US adults (54 % of those in their forties) had a parent aged 65 or more and a minor child, or had supported an adult child financially in the past year.
  - Pew Research Center (2026). More than half of Americans in their 40s are "sandwiched" between an aging parent and their own children (short read, 27 August; 8,750 adults). https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/08/27/more-than-half-of-americans-in-their-40s-are-sandwiched-between-an-aging-parent-and-their-own-children/ — survey (it measures who is sandwiched, not what they do for whom).
- **The noticing and reminding fall on one person, mostly the mother** (Daminger 2019; Robertson et al. 2019; Offer 2014; Ciciolla & Luthar 2019; Neustaedter et al. 2009: [agendas.md](agendas.md) §2.1, §2.3, findings 48–49). The same mechanism holds for a parent's papers: whoever knows when the parent's identity card ends is the one who renews it.

### 2.2 What helps, and what carers do instead
- **Formal proxy access is little used; carers borrow the person's identity.** At Geisinger, which had offered shared portal access since 2003, only about 0.4 % of adult portal users had a proxy; among 389 care partners with one (mostly parents, adult children and spouses), 23.3 % still logged in with the patient's own credentials. Patients were far likelier than their care partners to have inadequate health literacy (54.8 % against 8.8 %).
  - Wolff JL, Berger A, Clarke D, et al. (2016). Patients, care partners, and shared access to the patient portal: online practices at an integrated health system. *JAMIA* 23(6):1150–1158. https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocw025 — survey (one health system; figures from the abstract).
  - **Reading**: the household's admin runs through the person's own accounts and the dependent's borrowed ones. Sioul cannot fix the portals; it can keep *which account is whose* ("Odile's ameli account, which you open for Odile"), so that a login used for someone else is never mistaken for the person's own, and the dependent's password is never Sioul's to store unasked.
- **Trusted helpers can also exploit.** In a US national telephone survey of 5,777 adults aged 60 or more, 5.2 % reported financial exploitation by a family member in the past year.
  - Acierno R, Hernandez MA, Amstadter AB, et al. (2010). Prevalence and correlates of emotional, physical, sexual, and financial abuse and potential neglect in the United States: the National Elder Mistreatment Study. *American Journal of Public Health* 100(2):292–297. https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2009.163089 — survey.
  - **Reading**: this is why the CRPD asks for safeguards "free of conflict of interest" (§1.2), and why the tutelle owes accounts. Sioul should keep a dated record of the *acts* done for a person (a paper renewed, a payment made from their account), which the person can show the parent or a judge, and never a record of the parent's own movements or messages.
- **Reading, for the whole section**: the load is (1) knowing what each person's papers and dates are, (2) doing the steps through portals made for one adult, (3) remembering and reminding. Sioul already carries (1) for the person's own papers and (3) for children's events. A person object extends both to every member of the household, at no extra cost to the person: the papers' rules, reminders and renewal tasks apply unchanged, with a name on them.

## 3. The privacy and autonomy of the person cared for

### 3.1 Data about others, kept by one person: the household exemption and its limits
- **The GDPR does not apply to "a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity"** (art. 2(2)(c)). Recital 18: such activities "could include correspondence and the holding of addresses"; but the Regulation "applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities".
  - Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), art. 2(2)(c), recital 18. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj — legal text.
- **The Court reads "purely" narrowly.** Publishing data on the internet "so that those data are made accessible to an indefinite number of people" is not household activity (*Lindqvist*, C-101/01, 2003, §47); a home camera that also films the public street is not "purely" household (*Ryneš*, C-212/13, 2014, §§29–33); door-to-door preaching notes taken by members for a community are not either (*Jehovan todistajat*, C-25/17, 2018).
  - CJEU, C-101/01 *Lindqvist*, ECLI:EU:C:2003:596; C-212/13 *Ryneš*, ECLI:EU:C:2014:2428; C-25/17 *Jehovan todistajat*, ECLI:EU:C:2018:551. https://curia.europa.eu — legal text (court rulings; paragraphs from memory of the rulings, the texts not fetched here).
- **Reading**: a person keeping their child's or parent's papers on their own devices, sealed between their own devices, is the textbook household case, and Sioul's author provides software, not a service that receives the data: nothing Sioul does makes it a controller. Two things leave the household and so lose the exemption's shelter: (1) **an AI agent**, whose provider receives what it reads (docs/mcp.md: "What an agent reads goes to the provider of its model"); (2) **a server shared with others**, such as a CardDAV address book another person reads. Hence: a person's details are closed to agents by default, health and identity numbers never reach them (as health already never does), and nothing of a person goes to a server unless the person puts it there.

### 3.2 Minors' data
- **The GDPR gives children "specific protection"** (recital 38) and sets the age of consent to online services between 13 and 16 (art. 8); France chose 15 (Loi Informatique et Libertés, art. 45). Health data are a special category (art. 9), which the household exemption covers but an AI provider would not.
  - GDPR art. 8, 9, recital 38 (above). Loi n° 78-17 du 6 janvier 1978, art. 45. https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/JORFTEXT000000886460 — legal text.
- **Surveillance of children should not be routine, indiscriminate or unknown to them.** The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child: digital surveillance of children "should not be conducted routinely, indiscriminately or without the child's knowledge" (or, for very young children, their parent's), with a right to object, and the least privacy-intrusive means considered (General comment No. 25, §75). The explanatory notes to it add that tools that "monitor children's devices, track their location" should be used "sparingly and only for well-defined purposes", since they may "interfere with a child's development by creating the impression that they are never alone".
  - Committee on the Rights of the Child (2021). General comment No. 25 on children's rights in relation to the digital environment, CRC/C/GC/25, §75. https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3906061 — legal text (treaty body; read in the 5Rights Foundation's explanatory notes, which quote the paragraph). 5Rights Foundation (2024). Explanatory notes on General comment No. 25, notes to §§74–77. https://5rightsfoundation.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/ExplanatoryNotes_UNCRCGC25.pdf — practitioner.
- **The CNIL: proportionate to age and maturity, transparent to the child, no permanent location tracking.** Its recommendation 5 on parental control asks for "a principle of proportionality taking into account the child's interest, age and maturity", "transparency towards the child, clearly informing them", "avoiding intrusive devices such as permanent geolocation", and warns that intrusive monitoring can "alter the relationship of trust between parents and the minor".
  - CNIL (2021). Recommandation 5 : promouvoir des outils de contrôle parental respectueux de la vie privée et de l'intérêt de l'enfant. https://cnil.fr/fr/recommandation-5-promouvoir-des-outils-de-controle-parental-respectueux-de-la-vie-privee-et-de — official (a regulator's guidance).
- **Children dislike monitoring apps, and more control goes with more risk.** Of 736 Google Play reviews of 37 online-safety apps written by children aged 8 to 19, 76 % gave one star; children found the apps "overly restrictive and invasive of their personal privacy, negatively impacting their relationships with their parents". In a survey of 215 parent–teen pairs (teens 13–17), parents low in autonomy-granting used monitoring apps most, and more parental control went with more, not fewer, online risks.
  - Ghosh AK, Badillo-Urquiola K, Guha S, LaViola JJ, Wisniewski PJ (2018). Safety vs. surveillance: what children have to say about mobile apps for parental control. *CHI 2018*, paper 124. https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173698 — qualitative (app reviews).
  - Ghosh AK, Badillo-Urquiola K, Rosson MB, Xu H, Carroll JM, Wisniewski PJ (2018). A matter of control or safety? Examining parental use of technical monitoring apps on teens' mobile devices. *CHI 2018*, paper 194. https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173768 — survey (correlational: it does not show that the apps cause the risk).

### 3.3 Elderly parents: the children want monitoring more than the parents do
- **In interviews with 18 older women and 10 of their adult children**, the children preferred location tracking, in-home sensors and cameras more than their mothers did, underestimated their mothers' grasp of the technology and the importance of involving them, and were mostly confident they could persuade them. Privacy was the first concern of both, bound up with independence, identity and freedom; the authors call for shared decision-making "to prevent conflict and caregiver overreach".
  - Berridge C, Wetle TF (2020). Why older adults and their children disagree about in-home surveillance technology, sensors, and tracking. *The Gerontologist* 60(5):926–934. https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/gnz068 — qualitative.

### 3.4 Partners: the closest people are the hardest threat
- **Intimate threats break the usual defences**: people close to us "can answer our secret questions", reach our devices and "exercise coercive power over us"; the class spans partners, family and caregiving relationships, and relationships change (an ex keeps what a partner had).
  - Levy K, Schneier B (2020). Privacy threats in intimate relationships. *Journal of Cybersecurity* 6(1):tyaa006. https://doi.org/10.1093/cybsec/tyaa006 — theory.
- **Abusers use ordinary features, not hacking**: survivors and the professionals who help them describe abusers who own or share the victim's accounts and devices, and turn family plans and location sharing into monitoring.
  - Freed D, Palmer J, Minchala D, Levy K, Ristenpart T, Dell N (2018). "A stalker's paradise": how intimate partner abusers exploit technology. *CHI 2018*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174241 — qualitative (focus groups and interviews; the details are from secondary summaries, unverified at the paper).
- **Reading**: any person record that a partner can read on another device, any "family" account whose owner sees the others, any location share, is a tool for control the day the relationship turns. Sioul's persons live on the person's own devices only, sealed with the person's own key, and nothing about a partner is ever *shown to* that partner's devices or anyone else's by Sioul.

### 3.5 A dependent's own view of what is kept about them
- **Nothing in law gives a household member a right to see what a relative keeps** (the GDPR's access right, art. 15, does not reach household activity). But the CNIL asks for transparency to the child, the CRPD for safeguards and review, and the tutelle for "clear information, adapted to their state" (§1.2).
- **Reading (inference)**: Sioul should make *showing* easy, never automatic: on each person's page, "What Sioul keeps about Léa" lists their papers, agendas and letters and makes one file (PDF or a folder) the person can hand to them, at eighteen for a child, at any time for a parent or a partner. Handing over is the person's act; Sioul never sends it.

### 3.6 Care without control: the ethic of care
- **Care has phases, and the last is the cared-for's response.** Fisher and Tronto define care as "everything that we do to maintain, continue, and repair our 'world' so that we can live in it as well as possible"; Tronto's phases are *caring about* (attentiveness: noticing a need), *taking care of* (responsibility), *care-giving* (competence) and *care-receiving* (responsiveness: whether the care met the need, as the cared-for says). Tronto warns of paternalism, the carer deciding for the cared-for what they need, and of "privileged irresponsibility", those who can pass the work to others. *Caring with* (2013) adds trust and solidarity: care done together, over time.
  - Fisher B, Tronto J (1990). Toward a feminist theory of caring. In Abel EK, Nelson MK (eds.), *Circles of Care*, 35–62. SUNY Press — theory.
  - Tronto JC (1993). *Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care*. New York: Routledge — theory.
  - Tronto JC (2013). *Caring Democracy: Markets, Equality, and Justice*. New York: NYU Press — theory.
- **Dependency work is work, and the dependency worker needs care too.** Kittay: everyone is "some mother's child"; those who care for dependants become dependent in turn and are owed support (*doulia*).
  - Kittay EF (1999). *Love's Labor: Essays on Women, Equality, and Dependency*. New York: Routledge — theory.
- **Reading**: the four phases map onto Sioul's work for a person. *Attentiveness*: Sioul notices that Léa's identity card ends (the papers' rule), so the person does not have to. *Responsibility*: one holder per thing, as for children's events (finding 49). *Competence*: the steps known for each kind of paper. *Responsiveness*: what is kept is visible to the cared-for on request (§3.5), and a parent's own word ("I'll do it myself") ends Sioul's part, said once and kept. What care forbids: watching (no location, no "last seen", no reading of the dependent's messages), deciding for (no capacity flag, no automatic action in the dependent's name), and tallying (no count of what the person did for whom, finding 51).

## 4. The household notions French papers follow
Each administration draws its own household, by its own rules and its own ages. None of them is the household Sioul should keep.

- **Health insurance (ameli): a child is a "bénéficiaire rattaché" of one parent or both until 16; adults are insured in their own right.** Since the universal health protection (PUMa, 2016) an adult is no longer anyone's *ayant droit*; a minor is attached to one parent's or both parents' file and appears on their cards (both, "until the eve of their 16th birthday", when attached twice). A card of the child's own can be asked from 12, from a parent's ameli account; at 16 the insurer writes to the child, who gets their own card and may be reimbursed on their own account; at 18 the child has their own ameli account. The *attestation de droits* lists who is attached.

  - **Hand-over dates**: from the birth date, one quiet line on a child's page at 16 ("From 16, Tom can have a carte Vitale and a reimbursement account in Tom’s own name") and at 18 ("Tom is 18: the papers are Tom’s own, and can be handed over"), each once, in the admin window, never a reminder that nags.

## 5. Data model standards, and what servers keep

### 5.1 What each standard says
- **vCard 4.0 (RFC 6350)**: `KIND` is "individual", "group", "org" or "location" (§6.1.4); `MEMBER` "MUST NOT be present unless the value of the KIND property is 'group'" (§6.6.5); `RELATED` (§6.6.6) holds "a single URI", or a text, with a `TYPE` from "contact", "acquaintance", "friend", "met", "co-worker", "colleague", "co-resident", "neighbor", "child", "parent", "sibling", "spouse", "kin", "muse", "crush", "date", "sweetheart", "me", "agent", "emergency" (XFN's words, plus three); its example is `RELATED;TYPE=friend:urn:uuid:f81d4fae-…`. `UID` is "a globally unique identifier corresponding to the entity associated with the vCard" (§6.7.6). A relation is written on one card and says what the *other* is to the card's subject: whose card holds it decides its meaning.
  - Perreault S (2011). vCard Format Specification, RFC 6350. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6350 — standard.
- **JSContact (RFC 9553, May 2024)**, vCard's JSON successor: `kind` adds "device" and "application"; `members` is a map of member UIDs; `relatedTo` "is a map, where each key is the uid property value of the related Card", whose `relation` keys start from RFC 6350's TYPE values.
  - Stepanek R, Loffredo M (2024). JSContact: A JSON Representation of Contact Data, RFC 9553. https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9553 — standard.
- **schema.org's Person**: `parent`, `children`, `sibling`, `spouse`, `relatedTo` ("the most general family relation"), `knows`, `colleague`, `follows`, each expecting a Person. https://schema.org/Person — standard (community vocabulary).
- **FHIR's RelatedPerson** (R5): someone involved in a patient's care "who is not the target of care", with a "personal relationship or non-healthcare-specific professional relationship"; it holds `patient` (required), `relationship` (codes from the "Patient Relationship Type" set, HL7 v3 role codes such as family members, guardian and power of attorney: the codes from memory, unverified), `period` ("when the relationship is or was active"), `active`, and its own name, contacts and birth date. A Person resource links the records of one human across roles.
  - HL7 International (2023). FHIR R5, RelatedPerson. https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/relatedperson.html — standard.
  - **Reading**: FHIR is the one model that gives a relation a **period** and keeps it apart from the human's own record. A household relation has the same shape: who, to whom, what kind, since when, until when.

### 5.2 What the big address books keep
- **Google People API**: a `Relation` is `person`, "the name of the other person this relation refers to" (a string, not a reference), and `type`: "spouse", "child", "mother", "father", "parent", "brother", "sister", "friend", "relative", "domesticPartner", "manager", "assistant", "referredBy", "partner", or free text. Groups are contact-group memberships, not cards. Google's CardDAV speaks vCard 3.0 only ("Google uses VCard 3.0 as the contact encoding format"), which has no RELATED, KIND or MEMBER; what it keeps of them was not found documented.
  - Google (2026). People API, `people` resource, Relation. https://developers.google.com/people/api/rest/v1/people ; CardDAV API. https://developers.google.com/people/carddav — vendor.
- **Apple Contacts**: "related names" are a labelled list of names (spouse, partner, child, father, mother, parent, brother, sister, friend, assistant, manager), exported as `X-ABRELATEDNAMES` with an `X-ABLabel`; they are text, and do not link to the related card.
  - Apple. ABPersonRelatedNamesProperty. https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/addressbookui/abpersonrelatednamesproperty.md ; Apple Support Community, "Related Names in iOS Contacts don't link to the corresponding contacts". https://discussions.apple.com/thread/255705375 — vendor and a user forum (the export lines from a client's forum, https://forum.emclient.com/t/syncing-of-contacts-related-names/36701: unverified).
- **Nextcloud**: the server (sabre/dav) stores a card as given; asked for vCard 3.0, its converter turns `KIND:group` into Apple's `X-ADDRESSBOOKSERVER-KIND:GROUP` and `MEMBER` into `X-ADDRESSBOOKSERVER-MEMBER` and back, and passes `RELATED` through unchanged. The Contacts app reads `KIND` and Apple's form, leaves group cards out of the list (its groups are `CATEGORIES`), and edits `RELATED` as "Related contacts", a name typed as text with a type among Contact, Agent, Emergency, Friend, Colleague, Co-worker, Line manager, Assistant, Spouse, Child, Mother, Father, Parent, Brother, Sister, Relative (several outside RFC 6350's list); a separate field "Relationship to you" chooses one of Spouse, Child, Mother, Father, Parent, Brother, Sister, Relative, Friend, Colleague, Line manager, Assistant.
  - sabre/vobject, `lib/VCardConverter.php` (master). https://github.com/sabre-io/vobject/blob/master/lib/VCardConverter.php — code. Nextcloud Contacts, `src/models/rfcProps.js` (last changed 12 August 2026), `src/models/contact.js`, `src/store/contacts.js` (`sortContacts` filters `kind !== 'group'`). https://github.com/nextcloud/contacts/tree/main/src/models — code (read 4 November 2026; the line the "Relationship to you" field writes was not checked).
- **Sioul itself** writes new cards in vCard 3.0 and drops `KIND`, `MEMBER`, `RELATED` and the other 4.0-only lines for servers that take only 3.0 (`contacts::as_vcard3`, §0); it reads no group card ([compatibility.md](../compatibility.md), "Not read").

### 5.3 What it means for Sioul
- **No server keeps a relation as a reference that every client follows.** Google keeps a name and a type; Apple a name and a label; Nextcloud a typed text (or a URI, if one is written), each with its own words for the types. A relation written into a card is read as text everywhere else, or dropped.
- **A relation in a card is public to whoever reads the address book**: a shared address book, a phone's contacts app and every app it lets read them, Google. Household structure ("Léa: child", "Odile: mother") is exactly what the intimate-threat literature (§3.4) says leaks.
- **A person need not have a card, and a card is not a person**: a baby has no address or number; a partner has a card for reaching them, and a person record for the lease and the tax notice; one human may have two cards (home and work, or one in each address book).
- **Reading, the recommendation**: **a person is Sioul's own sealed record, linked to zero or more cards by their UIDs**, never written into a card. Its relation to the person carries a type from RFC 6350's own words (`child`, `parent`, `spouse`, `kin`, `co-resident`, `agent`, `emergency`, plus Sioul's `partner`, which RFC 6350 lacks), so that an export or a later choice to write `RELATED` needs no translation; and, like FHIR's, a `since` and an `until`. Nothing goes to CardDAV unless the person, on a person's page, chooses "Write this in the card" for one relation (later phase; written as `RELATED;TYPE=child;VALUE=uri:urn:uuid:<card UID>` in vCard 4.0 cards only).

## 7. Summary of findings
- **Admin is done for others, and it weighs.** One adult in four delays care because of health admin (Kyle & Frakt 2021); carers who coordinate care and medicines are five times likelier to give up valued activities (Wolff et al. 2016); in France 9.3 million people help a relative, and each does more each year (DREES 2023, 2026); a quarter of US adults are sandwiched between a parent and a child (Pew 2026). The noticing and reminding fall on one person ([agendas.md](agendas.md), findings 48–49).
- **Each relation gives Sioul a different mandate.** A child: kept *for* them, with a duty to their privacy and a hand-over written in law (Code civil art. 371-1, 414; ameli at 16; tax and CAF at 20, 21 and 25). An elderly parent: an adult who decides; the helper supports, never substitutes (CRPD art. 12; the Committee's 2021 observations on France); France's legal forms are dated measures, from a revocable procuration to a five-year tutelle. A partner: an equal; what the household shares, not what they own. The other parent: a person *of a child's*, outside the household.
- **The law shelters what stays in the household, and nothing that leaves it.** The GDPR does not apply to "purely personal or household" activity (art. 2(2)(c)), read narrowly (*Lindqvist*, *Ryneš*); an AI provider or a shared server is outside it. Minors' data, and health above all, deserve "specific protection" (recital 38); some of an adolescent's care is secret from the parents by law (CSP L1111-5).
- **Monitoring harms the cared-for and serves control.** Children rate monitoring apps one star and more control goes with more risk (Ghosh et al. 2018); adult children want sensors and tracking more than their mothers do (Berridge & Wetle 2020); intimate abusers use ordinary family features (Freed et al. 2018; Levy & Schneier 2020); the UN and the CNIL ask for no routine or permanent tracking, and transparency to the child (GC 25 §75; CNIL rec. 5).
- **Care, read through Tronto, has a last phase that belongs to the cared-for**: their response. Software that cares notices and takes on the work; it does not decide for, watch, or tally.
- **No standard or server keeps a relation as a reference every client follows.** vCard's RELATED is a typed URI or text on one card; Google keeps a name and a type, Apple a labelled name, Nextcloud a typed text; Google's CardDAV is vCard 3.0, which has no RELATED; FHIR alone gives a relation a period.
- **Family apps take one of two models**: an account per person in a group, whose owner gains power over the others (Apple, Google, Life360, FamilyWall, Cozi), or a profile kept by one person (OurFamilyWizard's children, Trustworthy's family members). Only the second fits a household's admin; none plans the hand-over or shows the dependent what is kept.

## 8. Recommendations for Sioul
Each names its evidence; "inference" marks those that rest on reasoning, not on a source. Built by 5 November 2026: all eleven, 10 by what is left unbuilt.

1. **A person is Sioul's own record, distinct from a contact, linked to zero or more cards by UID** (§5.3: no server keeps relations as references; a baby has no card; one human may have two cards). Never written into a card by default (§3.4, §5.2).
2. **Keep few fields: a name, a full name as papers write it, the relation, a birth date, whether they live with the person, the cards, and the relation's start and end** (§4 "carry"; FHIR's period, §5.1). Never a social security, identity or tax number (§4 "never"), never a health field, never a location.
3. **The relation, from the person's side, in RFC 6350's words where they exist**: child, parent, partner (RFC 6350 has spouse and sweetheart, not partner: Sioul's word), kin, another parent of a child, someone the person helps; and the person themself (vCard's "me"). Words, not statuses: "à charge", "ayant droit" and the foyer fiscal stay in the papers that state them (§4).
4. **Persons stay on the person's own devices, sealed**: in Sioul's data folder, not the notes folder a sync app may carry in clear; shared between the person's devices by Sioul's own sealed sharing only (§3.1, §3.4) — inference for the place.
5. **Persons are closed to AI agents by default, each with its own switch; health papers and identity numbers never reach agents, whatever the switch** (§3.1: the household exemption ends at the provider; §3.2: minors' and health data).
6. **An adult is never marked as unable to decide.** A legal measure (procuration, mandat de protection future, habilitation familiale, curatelle, tutelle) is a paper with its dates and its review, linked to the person who holds it and the person it is for, reminded as papers are (§1.2: CRPD art. 12, the 2021 observations; Code civil art. 415, 428).
7. **A partner's own papers enter only with the partner's word, recorded once as an act; at a separation, the person ends the relation, and Sioul offers to hand their papers back** (§1.3, §3.4) — inference.
8. **Nothing of a person is ever shown to anyone by Sioul; showing is the person's act.** Each person's page makes one file of what Sioul keeps about them, to hand over (§3.5: CNIL transparency, CRPD review, the tutelle's information duty; Tronto's care-receiving) — inference for the form.
9. **Hand-over dates said once**: at 16 and 18 for a child, one line on their page in the admin window (§4: ameli at 16, majority at 18); nothing counted, nothing nagged.
