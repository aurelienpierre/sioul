# Devices: what the research says about a device for work, one for admin, one for leisure

**The question**: does research support, or contradict, saying what each device is for (work, personal admin, leisure) and bringing to each device only what it is for, at the hours for it? What was built from it is in [areas.md](../areas.md#what-each-device-is-for).

Researched on 17 November 2026. Each finding was written down as soon as its source was checked, so the order is the order of checking, not of weight. A summary by strength, and what contradicts the design, is at the end.

**How to read each finding**: the claim; the reference with its DOI; **Read** says what was actually read (full text, abstract only, or a secondary summary) and where; **Sample and method**; **For the feature** is the design inference, which is Sioul's and not the paper's.

**Already in Sioul's notes, not repeated here**: Sonnentag & Fritz and the detachment findings ([research.md](../research.md), finding 21; [done-for-the-day.md](done-for-the-day.md), §2). Several sources below are already cited there in one line each (Park, Fritz & Jex 2011; Derks, van Mierlo & Schmitz 2014; Kreiner, Hollensbe & Sheep 2009 in done-for-the-day.md; Kreiner 2006, Kossek et al. 2012, Wepfer et al. 2018 and Gadeyne et al. 2018 in [global-pause.md](global-pause.md), §2.3, from their abstracts). They come back here only for what they say about devices, with what was read this time.

**Grades**, as in the other notes: meta (meta-analysis or systematic review), experiment, diary/field, correlational (cross-sectional survey), qualitative (interviews), theory.

## Findings

1. **People already use separate devices to keep work and the rest apart, and those who segment do it more; the study does not show that it works.**
   - Each person listed up to 10 devices and said whether each was used entirely for work, mainly for work, for both, mainly for non-work or entirely for non-work: close to what Sioul's switches record, with two domains where Sioul has three. A device separation score (1 = every device for both, 3 = every device for one side only) differed between the six boundary styles found by cluster analysis (F(5, 254) = 8.0, p < 0.01): "fusion lovers", who integrate with high control, scored 2.05, against 2.60 for "family firsters", 2.61 for "dividers" and 2.68 for "captives", the three segmenting styles. The number of devices used did not differ between styles: segmenters do not own more devices, they dedicate them.
   - The authors say themselves that this does not prove the tactic effective: "This could suggest that using separate devices creates more separation between home and work and decreases interruptions, and that it is an effective boundary management tactic. Alternatively, it could just be that people who prefer to segment their work and non-work lives are more likely to use device separation as a tactic." The study measured behaviours, not preferences and not outcomes (no stress, detachment or satisfaction measure).
   - They add that people with low control over their boundaries might gain from changing tactic ("Job Warriors could benefit from using separate devices for home and work, or, if they are able, captives could try using their devices across home and work more"), and that under bring-your-own-device policies "some people may want to buy a separate device for work/school in order to stay in control of their boundaries". Both are suggestions, not results.
   - Fleck R, Cox AL, Robison RAV (2015). Balancing boundaries: using multiple devices to manage work-life balance. *Proc. CHI 2015*, 3985–3988. https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702386 — correlational.
   - **Read**: full text, the authors' accepted version in the Anglia Ruskin repository (https://aru.figshare.com/articles/conference_contribution/Balancing_Boundaries_Using_Multiple_Devices_to_Manage_Work-Life_Balance/23762130; 4 pages, a CHI note). Figure 3 was read as its caption and the text's means, not as the drawing.
   - **Sample and method**: 285 UK workers from a web panel, selected to represent the UK working population by age, gender and region (37 self-employed among them); 26 who used no device regularly were excluded, which leaves 259 (the test's degrees of freedom imply 260). One questionnaire: device list with use, and Kossek's 17-item Work-Life Indicator; k-means clustering into six styles.
   - **What the title suggests and the paper does not say**: it does not report what "works or fails". It reports who dedicates devices. It is short, cross-sectional and self-reported.
   - **For the feature**: saying what a device is for puts into the software something a representative sample of workers already does by hand, and the person keeps the choice, since integrators dedicate their devices less: the switches must stay optional and a device with nothing said must keep receiving everything.

2. **Within one device too, people build "micro-boundaries": separate applications per account, work mail removed from the phone during time off, notifications off outside office hours; and mail read on one device gets lost for the other.**
   - Of 16 university employees, 11 read both work and personal mail on their phone (all 9 academics, 2 of 7 professional services staff); the other 5 professional services staff had only personal mail on their phone. Of the 11, 5 used separate applications for the two (one used two browser bookmarks instead of an app, because "Sometimes it's best not to know all the time if you have an unread message"), and 4, all academics, used one application for both.
   - All but one had work mail notifications on their computer, and more than half chose not to be interrupted by mail notifications on their phone: off entirely, off at certain hours, without sound or pop-up, or kept for particular senders or accounts.
   - The authors name these tactics micro-boundaries, "a strategy to limit the impact of micro-role transitions caused by cross-domain technology mediated interruptions", and list: personal and work mail on the same device in separate applications, "thereby limiting the temptation of checking work emails during non-work time"; "deliberately removing work email from their phone during time off, e.g. when on holiday"; folders with automatic filtering. All but three participants kept personal and work accounts apart while at work.
   - Dedicated devices did not hold the boundary alone: "The interviews revealed situations where it was not possible to maintain clear boundaries between work and personal email, despite using devices for only one type of account or not checking emails at certain times" (deadlines, colleagues who are not on holiday at the same time, part-time work). Two people's accounts leaked: a personal Gmail that brought work notifications through shared documents, and family writing to the work address "because they know that I see it more often".
   - A cost of several devices: mail scanned on the phone in the evening was marked read and then forgotten at the office ("because they're not marked as unread anymore, whenever I go to work I forget to reply to them"); a filter present on one client and absent on the other made extra work on the other.
   - Their design recommendations: "smart-notifications that allow users to decide when to be notified and from which account, based on their location", "especially important for users who prefer having all accounts synchronised to a single mobile application"; and marking mail with the device where it was first opened.
   - Cecchinato ME, Cox AL, Bird J (2015). Working 9-5? Professional differences in email and boundary management practices. *Proc. CHI 2015*, 3989–3998. https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702537 — qualitative, with a small questionnaire.
   - **Read**: full text, the published version in City's repository (https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/16618/1/working9-5.pdf).
   - **Sample and method**: 16 employees of one London university (9 academics, 7 professional services; 11 women; ages 20–54), a convenience sample; semi-structured interviews of 30–60 minutes, two questionnaires afterwards. The authors call the study exploratory and the sample small; the split between "permeable" academics and "rigid" professional services rests on 16 people in one institution.
   - **For the feature**: three things. What a device is for should act on what is told as well as on what is shown, since notifications are where these people drew the line. A person with everything on one device needs the same rule by hour on that device, which is the cell of the grid. And what was seen on one device must not look handled on the other: a thing folded or glanced at on the phone keeps its place on the computer, and the person must be able to open what is folded, since the boundary gives way for deadlines and urgent matters.

3. **Boundary tactics with technology are about who reaches whom and when (screening calls, a second mail address), not about separate devices; a boundary stronger than the person wants is a violation too, and emergencies pass through even strong boundaries.**
   - The paper classifies boundary work tactics as behavioural, temporal, physical and communicative. "Leveraging technology" is one of four behavioural tactics, described as "using technology to facilitate boundary work (e.g., voicemail, caller ID, e-mail)". The examples: caller ID to screen calls during non-work hours; a mobile number given "to only a select few"; an answering machine kept so as to hear each message and pick up only if it was urgent; asking people to write by e-mail instead of calling, which "gave several priests more flexibility as to when requests could be met"; and "some interviewees created multiple e-mail accounts, one for personal use, the other for parishioners to use". The paper adds that technology helps "if technology is actively managed".
   - **It does not mention separate devices.** The study dates from before smartphones (a Palm Pilot is the most recent device named). Under physical tactics, "managing physical artifacts", it reports separate calendars and separate key rings for work and home in some people and one of each in others, and postal mail kept from coming home.
   - Violations go both ways: an *intrusion* violation breaches a boundary the person wanted, and a *distance* violation is a boundary kept stronger than the person wanted. Proposition 2: people who want more segmentation perceive intrusions more often, and people who want more integration perceive distance violations more often.
   - "Allowing differential permeability": people "discriminate about precisely what will or will not pass through the work-home boundary, as well as which direction". Emergencies "were often cast as acceptable exceptions to otherwise strong segmentation norms, making them a kind of trump card". The authors write that earlier research "too often has oversimplified the way individuals manage the work-home boundary as either segmenting or integrating".
   - Kreiner GE, Hollensbe EC, Sheep ML (2009). Balancing borders and bridges: negotiating the work-home interface via boundary work tactics. *Academy of Management Journal* 52(4):704–730. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2009.43669916 — qualitative.
   - **Read**: full text, the copy on Boston College's Center for Work & Family site (https://www.bc.edu/content/dam/files/centers/cwf/rt/pdf/Kreiner.%202009.pdf); the technology, artifacts, differential permeability and violations sections read closely, the rest skimmed.
   - **Sample and method**: Episcopal parish priests in the United States, chosen as an extreme case of work entering home: written answers to open questions from 220 priests (study 1), then interviews with 60 priests, stratified by tenure and by whether they lived next to the church (study 2); grounded theory. One occupation; the model and its propositions are built from the interviews and not tested.
   - **What the title suggests and the paper does not say**: it is often cited for "separate devices and accounts" as tactics. It supports separate accounts and screening; separate devices are not in it.
   - **For the feature**: three things. The grid's cells, where one area is let through on a device at one time and not another, are differential permeability, and the paper says that is how people really manage, so a device is not simply "work" or "home". What is never held by a device's area (the person's Always-through people, codes just asked for, doses) is the trump card the priests kept. And for a person who integrates, a device that folds their leisure is a distance violation, a harm of its own: unsaid must mean everything, and the fold must open in one gesture.

4. **Asked in the moment, people want a notification on the device that is near them and in use; the phone is wanted nearly all day, a computer only while it is used, and a tablet hardly at all.**
   - Every 45 to 90 minutes for a week, each person rated, for a smartphone, a smartwatch, a tablet and a PC, "The mentioned device is in my proximity" and "I want to receive a notification on the mentioned device" (1 to 5), while the four devices logged their use. The wish to be notified differed by device (Friedman χ²(3) = 21.4, p < .001): smartphone 4.12, smartwatch 3.69, PC 2.56, tablet 1.63.
   - Within each person, the wish to be notified on a device rose with its proximity (mean r = 0.73 for the PC, 0.63 for the watch, 0.61 for the tablet, 0.45 for the phone, which was nearly always near) and with its time in active use (r = 0.51 for the PC, 0.37 for the tablet, 0.20 and 0.18 for watch and phone); a device lying still was wanted less. Place counted a little: PC and tablet were wanted less in transit and more at home.
   - The thesis's conclusion for design: "For device-independent notifications, one should focus on the last-used devices with currently active devices as the primary target for notifications", and PCs "received high ratings but only when in use. Using these types of devices to notify users while in use might reduce device-switching". It also warns that targeting a subset of devices needs a reliable implementation "to avoid triggering the fear of missing out by accidentally omitting notifications", and suggests "broadcasting important and urgent notifications to all devices, and reducing the number of targeted devices with decreasing importance and urgency". These are the author's recommendations; the study did not test a system that routes notifications.
   - In the companion interviews (another 16 people), tablet notifications were described as duplicates already seen on the phone, and few people changed any notification setting, even when they knew how: for some, dismissing each unwanted notification was "less effort" than configuring. None of the 16 had changed the defaults on their PC. The authors conclude that the default configuration is the essential question.
   - **The limit that matters here**: the question deliberately said nothing of what the notification was about ("we purposely did not specify details about the incoming notification"). The study says nothing on work against personal content, and nothing on hours. 11 of the 16 were students, 12 were men, the smartwatch was lent to all 16 and the tablet to 6, and 70 % of the answers were given at home.
   - Weber D, Voit A, Kratzer P, Henze N (2016). In-situ investigation of notifications in multi-device environments. *Proc. UbiComp 2016*, 1259–1264. https://doi.org/10.1145/2971648.2971732 — diary/field (experience sampling with device logs), small.
   - Companion: Voit A, Weber D, Henze N (2018). Qualitative investigation of multi-device notifications. *UbiComp 2018 Adjunct*, 1263–1270. https://doi.org/10.1145/3267305.3274117 — qualitative.
   - **Read**: the UbiComp paper itself is closed (ACM's page refused the request, no open copy is listed): its abstract was read in a search summary only. The study was read in full in the first author's doctoral thesis, whose chapter 5 says it is based on these two papers: Weber DC (2023), *Empirical assessment and improvement of ubiquitous notifications*, University of Stuttgart, §5.1–5.3, pp. 137–160 (https://elib.uni-stuttgart.de/handle/11682/15171). The numbers above are the thesis's; they were not compared with the 2016 paper's tables.
   - **Sample and method**: 16 people recruited from a university mailing list (18 enrolled, 2 excluded), 19 to 58 years old (mean 26), 11 students; 965 questionnaires over 7 days; correlations computed per person and averaged, with no test of a predictive model despite the abstract's "can be used to predict".
   - **For the feature**: three things. Among the devices where a thing comes forward, the one in use should tell it, which is what the "notices" lease read per area does. Routing must never drop silently: when no device that is for the thing is in use, it has to wait in view and be told later, and what is urgent (the list of what a device's area never holds) goes to every device. And since few people configure anything, the feature must do the right thing with nothing said, and saying what a device is for must take three switches once, not a table of settings per app.

5. **People differ in how they keep work and the rest apart, and what goes with better outcomes is feeling in control of the boundary, not separating or mixing as such.**
   - A cluster analysis of 591 managers (the cluster sizes of table 4 add up to 592) on five measures (non-work interrupting work, work interrupting non-work, boundary control, work identity, family identity) gave six profiles. Four have high control: "fusion lovers", who integrate both ways (n = 138, the largest group), "nonwork eclectics" (128), "family guardians" (126) and "dividers", who separate both ways (76). Two have low control: "overwhelmed reactors" (69) and "work warriors" (55). Dividers were about one in eight.
   - Boundary control "was the most consistent (and strongest) predictor of the study's outcomes": in the two samples it correlated with psychological distress (r = −.22 and −.41), intention to leave (−.21, −.28), time adequacy (.48, .45) and work-schedule fit (.51, .50). The low-control profiles scored higher on work-family conflict and distress, and the high-control ones lower, dividers and family guardians most clearly.
   - The authors' reading: "what matters more for positive work–family outcomes than the mere act of integrating or separating is whether one feels in control of boundaries", and "It is important to move beyond a simple distinction between integrators and segmenters." For practice: "Dividers, in contrast, need the ability to leave work at work and home at home", and flexibility "without boundary control may not necessarily be helpful".
   - Letting work interrupt the rest still had a cost of its own, whatever the profile: work-to-nonwork interruptions correlated with distress (r = .21 and .20) and with less time adequacy (−.31, −.25).
   - Kossek EE, Ruderman MN, Braddy PW, Hannum KM (2012). Work–nonwork boundary management profiles: a person-centered approach. *Journal of Vocational Behavior* 81(1):112–128. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2012.04.003 — correlational, with a cluster analysis.
   - **Read**: full text, the author's personal copy hosted by Purdue University (https://apps.it.purdue.edu/sites/Home/DirectoryApi/Files/b027a087-e6fe-4051-92c7-7a6c572c290b/Download); tables 4 to 7 read as text, cluster sizes from table 4. ([global-pause.md](global-pause.md), §2.3, cites this paper from its abstract.)
   - **Sample and method**: two samples of managers attending leadership courses at a US management education centre (278 and 313; mean age 44–45; mostly upper-middle and top managers of large multinationals), one online survey each; scales validated on the first sample and confirmed on the second, k-means clustering on both together. Cross-sectional and self-rated: the authors say they "cannot establish evidence of a causal relationship", and ask for replication outside the US, in small firms and among "entrepreneurs". The six-cluster solution was chosen as "the most interpretable", following earlier qualitative work.
   - **For the feature**: the feature must stay optional and adjustable, because the largest high-control group mixes work and the rest by choice and does well. What the software can add is control, so it should say plainly why a thing is folded on this device, let the person open it, and let any cell of the grid be changed; a device rule the person cannot see or override would lower control, which is the variable that tracked distress.

6. **People who prefer to keep work apart, and whose colleagues do, detach more in the evening, partly because they use work technology at home less.** *(Not read in full: abstract and a secondary summary.)*
   - The abstract: "segmentation preference and segmentation norm were positively associated with psychological detachment. Further, technology use at home partially mediated these relationships." The mediation is partial: using less work technology at home explains some of the link, not all of it.
   - From the secondary summary (the British Psychological Society's Research Digest, which gives no coefficient): 431 university alumni in full-time employment answered one survey; technology use was how often they used e-mail, the internet, a phone and a PDA for work at home; preference was measured with items such as "I prefer to keep work life at work". The digest notes that "technology did not explain all of the variance" and that "setting strict rules about technology use is not the only way to help psychological detachment, nor necessarily sufficient".
   - The paper measures how often work technology is used at home. Neither the abstract nor the digest mentions separate devices or accounts, and the data come from before smartphones were general.
   - Park Y, Fritz C, Jex SM (2011). Relationships between work-home segmentation and psychological detachment from work: the role of communication technology use at home. *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 16(4):457–467. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023594 — correlational.
   - **Read**: the abstract (PubMed, PMID 21728434) and the digest (https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/using-work-technology-home-hinders-our-ability-detach-work). The full text is behind APA's paywall; the copy on ResearchGate refused the request. The sample size and the measures are the digest's, not checked against the paper. (Already cited from its abstract in [done-for-the-day.md](done-for-the-day.md), §2.)
   - **Sample and method**: one cross-sectional self-report survey; the direction of cause is not established (people who detach easily may also be the ones who leave the laptop closed).
   - **For the feature**: it supports the mechanism the feature relies on (less work technology in reach at home goes with more detachment) and limits the promise: keeping work off the personal phone can help the evening, and will not by itself produce detachment.

7. **On days when people used their work smartphone more in the evening, they detached less, and felt more exhausted; the loss was sharper for those whose workplace expects work and home to be kept apart.** *(Not read in full: abstract and a later paper by the same last author.)*
   - The abstract: 70 employees "using smartphones on initiative of their employer" filled in a diary on 4 successive workdays (268 data points). The hypotheses, which "overall, the results of multilevel analyses supported": smartphone use for work goes with less detachment that day, less detachment goes with more exhaustion, and "especially employees who perceive a high segmentation norm at their workplace have difficulties to psychologically detach from work on days that they use their smartphone more intensively".
   - The direction of that last result is easy to misread, so it was checked in a second source. Van Laethem, van Vianen & Derks (2018, read in full) restate it: "Employees reporting a high segmentation norm at work had more trouble to psychologically detach from work when using their smartphone more intensively", and give the correlation between the segmentation norm and daily detachment in the 2014 study as r = 0.26. So a segmentation norm goes with more detachment in general, and with a larger drop on the days the boundary is crossed. The simple slopes and the figure of the 2014 paper were not read.
   - Van Laethem et al.'s own diary study (116 Dutch employees, 5 days, 476 data points, 89 % with higher education) found the same main effect: smartphone use for work after work predicted lower detachment that evening (γ = −0.18, p < .001), whatever the person's telepressure; segmentation preference, a control variable, correlated with daily detachment (r = 0.27).
   - Derks D, van Mierlo H, Schmitz EB (2014). A diary study on work-related smartphone use, psychological detachment and exhaustion: examining the role of the perceived segmentation norm. *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 19(1):74–84. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035076 — diary/field.
   - Van Laethem M, van Vianen AEM, Derks D (2018). Daily fluctuations in smartphone use, psychological detachment, and work engagement: the role of workplace telepressure. *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:1808. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01808 — diary/field, open access.
   - **Read**: for Derks et al. 2014, the abstract only (Erasmus University's repository record, https://repub.eur.nl/pub/66484, which holds no file; APA's page is paywalled; ResearchGate and CORE refused the requests). For Van Laethem et al. 2018, the full text on the publisher's site. (Both already cited in [done-for-the-day.md](done-for-the-day.md), §2, for the main effect only.)
   - **Sample and method**: both are small Dutch samples of employees who use a smartphone for work, self-reported every evening; all measures were taken at the same moment of the day, so the order of cause within a day is not established (the 2018 authors say so).
   - **Note on the title**: the "segmentation norm" is what the person perceives the workplace expects, not the person's own preference and not a separate device. All participants had one employer-provided smartphone; no one was compared with or without a dedicated work phone.
   - **For the feature**: two things. The main effect is the case for a work phone, or the work part of a phone, staying quiet in the evening. The moderation is a caution: once a boundary is the rule, crossing it costs more, so "Show anyway" on a leisure device in the evening is not free, and the feature should make crossing rarely necessary (what is urgent passes by itself) instead of making it easy and frequent. This second reading is Sioul's inference from a workplace norm to a personal rule; no study tested it.

8. **A leading researcher of boundary styles recommends separate devices as a way to gain control, and notes that synced devices duplicate work.** *(Expert advice in a practitioner journal, not a test.)*
   - Kossek's article for managers lists, among tactics to raise boundary control: "One effective strategy is to separate physical boundaries by having a separate communication device, such as one cell phone or tablet for work and one for nonwork. Another approach is to leave your work cell phone out of the bedroom at night so you won't be tempted to check work email as soon as you wake up". Its table of tactics has "Separate by using different devices for work and personal life", "Let others know when you are available and how to contact you during emergencies/critical times when unavailable" and "Turn off email and distracting devices for working periods".
   - On the cost of several devices: "often phones and laptops or tablets are synced, and email can be duplicated and thereby needs to be deleted on both personal and work devices."
   - It describes three styles (integrators, separators, and "cyclers" who alternate with the peaks of their work), each with a high-control and a low-control form, and says "integrators have higher technological dependence than separators" on a scale of Kossek's own; no sample or number is given for that statement in the article.
   - The article calls the separate device "effective" without citing a study that tested it. It also warns that "excessively high workload and role overload may mean that merely tinkering with boundary management styles might not improve outcomes."
   - Kossek EE (2016). Managing work-life boundaries in the digital age. *Organizational Dynamics* 45(3):258–270. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.orgdyn.2016.07.010 — expert review for practitioners (the "clinical model" grade of these notes: a specialist's framework, without trials).
   - **Read**: full text of the in-press manuscript on Boston College's Center for Work & Family site (https://www.bc.edu/content/dam/files/centers/cwf/rt/members/pdf/Kossek%20inpress10%20%20Managing%20Work-Life%20Boundaries%20in%20the%20Digital%20Age.pdf); the published version was not compared.
   - **For the feature**: the recommendation matches the design, and "cyclers" are a reason for the grid's cells to be easy to change and put back, since a person's style moves with the peaks of their work. The duplication Kossek describes is what "the device in use tells, once" removes. The reminder to say how to be reached in an emergency is the list of what a device's area never holds.

9. **For people who like to mix work and the rest, using the phone for work in the evening went with less conflict and a better evening at home; for people who prefer to keep them apart it changed nothing, because they hardly did it.** *(This is the finding that speaks most directly against making the feature a default.)*
   - Over four evenings, 71 Dutch employees reported how much they used their smartphone for work that evening, their work-family conflict and how they did in their family role. The person's segmentation preference (Kreiner's scale) moderated the link between smartphone use and conflict (γ = .54, SE = .10, p < .001). For integrators (low preference), more use went with less conflict that day (simple slope β = −1.21, p < .001) and, through it, with better family role performance on its relationship side. For segmenters the slope was not significant (β = .25).
   - The authors expected harm for segmenters and did not find it. Their explanation is the "highly significant negative correlation between segmentation preference and daily work-related smartphone use during off-job hours": "These segmenters might succeed in preventing work from intruding on their family domains by switching off their smartphone." In other words, segmenters had already drawn the line themselves.
   - Their conclusion: for integrators "the smartphone seems to facilitate their preferred lifestyle"; the relationship between smartphone use and conflict "is not as straightforward as we had previously thought".
   - The outcomes here are conflict and family role performance, not detachment or exhaustion: the same evening use that lowers an integrator's conflict may still cost recovery (findings 6 and 7, and Wepfer et al. 2018 in [global-pause.md](global-pause.md), §2.3). This study did not measure that.
   - Derks D, Bakker AB, Peters P, van Wingerden P (2016). Work-related smartphone use, work–family conflict and family role performance: the role of segmentation preference. *Human Relations* 69(5):1045–1068. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018726715601890 — diary/field.
   - **Read**: full text of the published article, on SAGE's companion site (https://study.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/work-related_smartphone_use.pdf); abstract, method, results and discussion read, the theory section skimmed.
   - **Sample and method**: 71 employees recruited through Facebook and LinkedIn (56 % men, mean age 38, 60 % with a degree, all with their work mail on their phone), 265–280 evening reports over 4 workdays, multilevel models. All daily measures were taken at bedtime, so "the temporal order of the variables could not be established" (the authors); self-report; one small convenience sample in one country.
   - **For the feature**: a device rule that kept work off an integrator's phone in the evening would take away something that, in this sample, went with less conflict at home. So nothing is narrowed until the person says what a device is for, a phone may be said to be for everything, and a cell of the grid may let work through at leisure hours. The people the feature serves are those who want the line and cannot hold it by switching the phone off, since on one phone work and everything else arrive together.

10. **Before smartphones were general, people already gave each device a role, and those in industry wanted "work is work, home is home" across devices; they could not hold it, and "carrying two phones makes little sense".**
   - 27 people who used several devices were interviewed. Of the 21 from industry, 14 said that separate work and home devices help ensure that "work is work, home is home". Their three reasons: the company's rules on where work data may be; "a personal desire to keep a boundary between work and personal/family time" ("If I am at my kid's games I don't want to be beeped. I am off the clock."); and a family computer being no safe place for work. The 6 from academia did not share the wish for separate devices and made everything reachable from everywhere.
   - The separation did not hold: "the boundary between work and home is stronger in theory than in practice. Thirteen of the 14 industry participants who desired to keep work and home devices distinct indicated that they do work tasks on home computers" ("If I want to work, [I use] whatever machine is handy."). It was "particularly problematic for mobile devices".
   - On phones: people used one phone for business and personal tasks ("carrying two phones makes little sense"; 2 of the 27 carried two), and because devices "only offer all-or-nothing synchronization", they had to put personal contacts on the work computer, work contacts on the personal one, or do without.
   - The authors' recommendations: "making devices aware of their roles (both within activities and as work or personal devices)" and "Devices should explicitly support separate work and personal roles". All 27 named managing information across devices as a significant problem, and the paper asks that synchronisation earn trust "through greater visibility: showing the user what will happen, what is happening, what has happened, and allowing them to easily undo an action".
   - Dearman D, Pierce JS (2008). "It's on my other computer!": computing with multiple devices. *Proc. CHI 2008*, 767–776. https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357177 — qualitative.
   - **Read**: full text, the first author's copy on the University of Toronto's site (https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~dearman/papers/2008-MultiDeviceComp-CHI.pdf); the sections on work and home, roles and the recommendations read closely, the sections on file transfer skimmed.
   - **Sample and method**: 27 people (19 men), 21 from IBM Research (8 of them summer interns) and 6 from a research group at Stanford University, interviewed at work in July–August 2007; highly educated and technical, as the authors note. Old: the phones were BlackBerrys and feature phones.
   - **For the feature**: three things. The wish for a device with a role is old and was stated by the people concerned, and the paper's own recommendation is the feature: a device that knows whether it is for work or personal. People will not carry two phones, so the same rule has to work by hour on one device (the grid's cells), not only by device. And since 13 of 14 crossed their own line when the other machine was handy, the fold must open ("Show anyway") without undoing the rule, and what was done on the "wrong" device must be there on the right one.

11. **Half of a group of professionals given a work smartphone wanted to keep work apart and could not; those who managed had decided how they would use the device before they got it.** *(Not read in full: abstract only.)*
   - The abstract: a "longitudinal case study of the adoption and use of a BlackBerry smartphone by 25 professional knowledge workers". At the second interview there were three groups: "Segmentors (n = 4) did not use their smartphones outside work hours. Integrators (n = 8), used their smartphones to connect to both work and family anywhere, but not any time (temporally separated work and family roles). Struggling segmentors (n = 13) felt pressured by their organization to use their device 24/7 and did so." Successful boundary management "depends on the development of a strategy to manage the device prior to adoption, the ability to change one's strategy to respond to concerns at home, and self-control."
   - Derks et al. (2016, finding 9, read in full) summarise the same study: the segmentors "did not see the advantages or need of a smartphone after work and felt that they had to protect their family life by reducing work-related smartphone use outside the work domain", and the integrators "reported that their work–life balance had improved".
   - Duxbury L, Higgins C, Smart R, Stevenson M (2014). Mobile technology and boundary permeability. *British Journal of Management* 25(3):570–588. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8551.12027 — qualitative, longitudinal.
   - **Read**: the abstract, as deposited with Crossref (the article is behind Wiley's paywall), and its summary in Derks et al. 2016. The interviews, the organisation and the time between the two waves were not read.
   - **Sample and method**: 25 knowledge workers, interviewed before and after adopting an employer's BlackBerry; small, one technology; the dates of the interviews were not read.
   - **For the feature**: three things. The largest group is the one the feature is for: people who want the line and lose it to a device that is always on. Even the successful integrators kept work and family apart in time ("anywhere, but not any time"), which is the grid's time axis and supports hours on a device said to be for everything. And what told the groups apart was a rule set beforehand, changeable, and not resting on self-control: a rule kept by the software is such a rule, to be set when a device joins, and easy to change later.

12. **Where employers encourage using one device for work and private life, people report more work-to-life conflict, and more so those who want to keep the two apart.** *(Not read in full: abstract only.)*
   - The abstract: "this dual-use of technologies comes at a price. It intensifies the blurring of the boundaries between work and private life — a development in stark contrast to the widely spread desire of employees to segment more clearly between their two lives." A survey found "a significant relationship" between organisational encouragement for dual use and work-to-life conflict; "in line with boundary theory, the effect is stronger for people that strive for work-life segmentation."
   - A companion case study in four organisations (ECIS 2015; known from a search summary of its abstract only) reports that people, "whether they strive for work-life integration or segmentation, often experience conflicts in realising their individual preference".
   - Köffer S, Junglas I, Chiperi C, Niehaves B (2014). Dual use of mobile IT and work-to-life conflict in the context of IT consumerization. *Proc. ICIS 2014*, Auckland. https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis2014/proceedings/EBusiness/7/ (no DOI) — correlational.
   - **Read**: the abstract on the AIS eLibrary's record page; the PDF was refused to an automated request (403), as was CORE's copy. Sample size, measures and coefficients were not read: they must be checked before any number from this paper is cited.
   - **For the feature**: one device for everything is the situation most people are in, and it goes with more conflict for those who want the line. The feature has to give the line on one shared device (by hour, by area), not only between two devices, and it matters most to segmenters, which again argues for a choice and not a default.

13. **A device bought without a purpose gets work fitted into it: tablets meant for leisure ended up carrying work mail at home.**
   - Of 82 office workers who used a tablet at least partly for work, 74 % used it for work and entertainment equally, 92 % read work mail on it, 87 % used it at home, and 63 % shared it with a partner, children or others. Of 47 who had had theirs for more than six months, 40 % used it more at home than at first.
   - In 11 interviews, most had got the tablet "despite having no prior plan as to how they were going to use them" ("I was trying to shoehorn it into everything. I was desperate to always check emails on it"); 8 attended to work mail or documents during rest time ("In the evenings I can do more ad-hoc work, because I don't have to go and switch my laptop or a computer on.").
   - The authors' reading: "The lack of clear understanding of their purpose encourages users to try and fit the devices into their lives, and may lead to increased work use"; "It is nearly impossible to separate personal and work use of a tablet"; and "the presence of familiar, work-related icons can invite users to interleave work and personal tasks. This may make it difficult to relax and recover after work". These are interpretations: the study measured no outcome of work-life balance, and says tablets "have the potential" to disturb it.
   - Stawarz K, Cox AL, Bird J, Benedyk R (2013). "I'd sit at home and do work emails": how tablets affect the work-life balance of office workers. *CHI 2013 Extended Abstracts*, 1383–1388. https://doi.org/10.1145/2468356.2468603 — correlational (descriptive survey) and qualitative; a six-page work-in-progress paper.
   - **Read**: full text, the authors' version in UCL's repository (https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1396455/).
   - **Sample and method**: an online questionnaire spread on Twitter and LinkedIn, 82 complete answers (76 % men, mean age 30), people using the tablet only for leisure excluded by design, so the sample cannot say how many tablets stay leisure devices; then 11 interviews with iPad users. Descriptive statistics only.
   - **For the feature**: this is the mechanism the feature answers. A device whose purpose was never said drifts toward work because work is one tap away on it, and seeing the work items is itself the cue. Saying "this tablet is for leisure" has to remove the work items from view there, not only silence them, and a shared family device is a further reason to keep work, and admin, out of sight on it.

14. **Taught to draw the line, people detached more, in a quasi-experiment and in a randomised trial; in the trial the gain was largest for those who prefer to mix.** *(Not read in full: abstracts only. Neither intervention used devices.)*
   - A two-day boundary management training in a large German company (37 trained, 27 controls) was followed two weeks later by "an increase in boundary creation and detachment, but, contrary to the hypothesis, a decrease in control during leisure time"; "No effects were found for the well-being variables."
   - A three-week online self-training teaching mindfulness "as a cognitive–emotional segmentation strategy", against a wait-list (190 participants, daily questionnaires), raised detachment and satisfaction with work-life balance and lowered psychological work-family conflict; "segmentation preferences moderated the intervention effect on psychological detachment, such that participants with low segmentation preference reported stronger intervention effects". The authors' practitioner point: the training "increases detachment from work most successfully for integrators".
   - Rexroth M, Feldmann E, Peters A, Sonntag K (2016). Learning how to manage the boundaries between life domains: effects of a boundary management intervention on boundary management, recovery, and well-being. *Zeitschrift für Arbeits- und Organisationspsychologie* 60(3):117–129. https://doi.org/10.1026/0932-4089/a000197 — quasi-experiment (not randomised), small.
   - Althammer SE, Reis D, van der Beek S, Beck L, Michel A (2021). A mindfulness intervention promoting work–life balance: how segmentation preference affects changes in detachment, well-being, and work–life balance. *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* 94(2):282–308. https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12346 — RCT (wait-list control), open access.
   - **Read**: both abstracts, as deposited with Crossref. Effect sizes, attrition and what the trainings contained in detail were not read. The second is a mental technique for leaving work thoughts, not a rule about devices or messages: it shows that segmenting can be learned and helps detachment, not that separating devices does.
   - **For the feature**: these are the only causal results found, and they point two ways. Drawing the line can raise detachment, and integrators are not exempt from the benefit, so the feature is worth offering to them too, as an offer. And in the quasi-experiment the trained group felt less control over their leisure afterwards: a boundary that feels imposed can cost the sense of control that finding 5 says matters most, so each rule must stay the person's, visible and reversible.

15. **Others already separate work from the rest on one phone and send a notice to the device in use; none of the pages read lets a person say what each of their devices is for.** *(Vendors' own help pages: they describe products, and measure nothing.)*
   - Android's work profile keeps work apps in a part of the phone that can be paused: "When your Work Profile is paused, work apps won't run, generate notifications, or consume data and battery life." It can follow a schedule: "your device will automatically turn on and off your Work Profile for the time periods that you have specified", by hours and days of the week, though scheduling "does not allow work apps to be paused continuously for consecutive days (i.e. over a weekend)". It is set up by an employer's management; it is one device with two parts, not a rule across devices (Google, Android Enterprise Help, "Pause or turn on your Work Profile"; read on 17 November 2026: https://support.google.com/work/android/answer/7029561).
   - Slack sends to the phone only once the computer is idle: "By default, you'll receive mobile notifications one minute after locking your desktop screen or 10 minutes after Slack stops detecting cursor activity", changeable to "Immediately, even if I'm active" or to a delay. It also has a notification schedule by hours and days, and separate preferences for mobile (Slack Help Center, "Configure your Slack notifications"; read on 17 November 2026: https://slack.com/help/articles/201355156-Configure-your-Slack-notifications). This is finding 4's rule (the device in use tells) as a shipped default.
   - Apple's Focus was **not reached**: every Apple Support address tried answered 404 or 304 to an automated request. A search summary of the iPhone User Guide gives "To share your Focus across your Apple devices, turn on Share Across Devices (you must be signed in with the same Apple ID on all your devices)", which would be the opposite choice, one state for every device; this was not read on Apple's page and must be read there before it enters a comparison table.
   - Not looked at: Microsoft Outlook's and Teams' quiet hours, Samsung's modes, Proton, Thunderbird. A tool's absence here means its pages were not read.
   - **For the feature**: the two halves exist separately (a work part of a phone that sleeps on a schedule; a notice that goes to the device in use), which makes each half less of a risk. Saying what each device is for, across a person's devices and for three areas, was not found on the pages read: the guide may say so in those words, and must not claim that nothing of the kind exists.

## Summary

### What is solid
- **People differ, and control matters more than the style** (finding 5; Kossek et al. 2012, read in full: 591 managers, two samples, the scale replicated). With Kreiner 2006 ([global-pause.md](global-pause.md), §2.3: what counts is the fit between wanted and actual segmentation), this is the firmest ground, and it is ground for the feature being **optional and visible**, not for the feature itself.
- **Work on the phone in the evening goes with less detachment that day** (finding 7: two Dutch diary studies, one read in full; and finding 21 of [research.md](../research.md)). Correlational within persons, replicated, same direction as the meta-analysis already cited there.
- **People do dedicate devices, and segmenters more** (finding 1; Fleck et al. 2015, read in full: 259 UK workers from a representative panel). Solid as a description of behaviour; it shows no benefit.
- **The device in use is where people want to be told** (finding 4; read in full in the first author's thesis). One small sample of mostly students, but the result is large within persons (r up to 0.73), and Slack ships it as a default.

### What is thin
- **That separate devices work.** No study found tests it. Fleck et al. say so themselves; Kossek 2016 calls it "effective" without a test; the qualitative studies (2, 10) report that dedicated devices did not hold the line alone. The only causal results (finding 14) are about trainings without devices, read as abstracts.
- **Device roles in practice** (findings 2, 10, 13): three small qualitative studies of university and research staff and tablet owners, 2007–2014. Good for what people say and do, and for the failure modes; no numbers to generalise.
- **Abstract-only sources**: Park et al. 2011 (6), Derks et al. 2014 (7, with its direction confirmed in a second paper), Duxbury et al. 2014 (11), Köffer et al. 2014 (12), Rexroth et al. 2016 and Althammer et al. 2021 (14). Each is marked. No number from Köffer may be cited: none was read.
- **The third area.** Every study found knows two domains, work and non-work. None separates personal admin from leisure, on devices or otherwise. Keeping admin off a leisure device is Sioul's inference, by analogy with work (admin as the unpaid work of finding 3 of [research.md](../research.md)), and must be stated as such.
- **Hours on a device**: supported by practice only (notifications turned off outside office hours in finding 2; integrators who separate "anywhere, but not any time" in finding 11; Android's schedule in finding 15). No study measures an outcome.
- **Everything is about employees.** Only finding 1 includes self-employed people (37 of 285), without analysing them apart; Kossek et al. ask for studies of "entrepreneurs". People who work for themselves from home, the ones [areas.md](../areas.md) names as most at risk, are not in this literature.

### What contradicts the feature, or limits it
Where Sioul stood on 17 November 2026 is said under each point; [areas.md](../areas.md#what-each-device-is-for) says how it works now.

1. **For integrators, the line can cost** (finding 9, read in full). In 71 employees, evening work on the phone went with *less* conflict at home for those who prefer to mix, and with nothing for segmenters, who had already cut it. Kreiner et al. (finding 3) name the harm: a boundary stronger than wanted is a "distance violation". **Built**: a device nothing is said of brings everything, and that stays true after the first device is classified: saying that the computer is for work does not make the phone lose work.
2. **Few people configure anything** (finding 4, the interviews). "Unsaid is everything" then means that most people never get the feature. The two findings pull apart, and one way to hold both would be to ask once, when a device joins, with three switches and a "for everything" that costs nothing (finding 11: what separated those who managed was a strategy set before the device arrived). **Not built**: Sioul does not ask when a device joins; the switches wait in Settings ▸ Devices.
3. **A dedicated device does not hold the line alone, and people cross it** (findings 2 and 10: 13 of 14 did work on the home computer). **Built**: every fold keeps its door, and the words at the fold are neutral, with no count of crossings, since crossing may cost more once a line is the rule (finding 7, by inference). Opening a fold settles nothing. **Not changed**: what is then done to a thing is done on every device, as before devices: a message read on a device that is not for it is read everywhere, so finding 2's cost (mail read on the phone, forgotten at the office) remains for mail that is read.
4. **The phone is where people want to be told, nearly all day; a computer only while in use** (finding 4). With a computer for work and a phone for admin and leisure, a work matter arriving during work hours while the person is away from the computer is told nowhere until the computer is used again. The thesis warns about exactly this ("fear of missing out by accidentally omitting notifications") and suggests that what is urgent goes to every device. **Built**: what is urgent or chosen is never kept back by a device, and nothing is dropped: a thing waits in view and is told by the first device it comes forward on that can. **Left to the person**: the ordinary work notice during work hours follows the cells; the phone's cell for work hours can be changed to let work through there, and is left as the device's areas say otherwise, though the evidence leans the other way, weakly.
5. **Technology tactics in the literature are about who and when, not which device** (finding 3: screening callers, a number given to a few, a second address; nothing on devices). Sioul already has those two axes (the senders let through, the hours). The device is the least evidenced of the three. **Built**: as its areas say, a device only narrows what the other two bring; only a cell the person changes can bring something the hour does not.
6. **Devices are shared** (finding 13: 63 % of work tablets were shared with family; two of ten own tablets in finding 4). On a shared device, folding is not privacy: the data is still there. **Not built**: data that never reaches a device; for a family tablet said to be for leisure it is closer to a need than to a refinement.
7. **Learning to draw the line lowered the sense of control over leisure in one small quasi-experiment** (finding 14, abstract only), while control is what tracks distress (finding 5). A weak signal, same direction as point 1. **Built**: every rule is shown in the grid, a fold says in one sentence that the device is the reason, and a cell goes back in one gesture.

### Method
Web requests on 17 November 2026 with a generic client name and no personal detail. Every DOI above was resolved on Crossref (title, authors, venue, pages), except Köffer et al. 2014, which has none. "Read in full" means the text was downloaded from the address given and its method, results and discussion were read; figures were read as their captions and the numbers in the text. Quotations are copied from the texts read. Searches were not exhaustive: no systematic review of device separation was looked for beyond the candidates given and their citations, and no study of the feature as designed exists to find.
